**SQLite**, unless you also have a server, in which case SQLite on the client and Postgres on the server. That pairing is the normal architecture for local-first, not a compromise between the two options.

Local-first means the app works with the network off, which means the database runs in your process on a machine you don't control. SQLite is that: one file, no daemon, no port, no version skew between what you shipped and what the user has installed. Postgres per client means an initdb, a data directory, a supervised process, and a major-version upgrade path on every machine you can't log into. Embedded Postgres distributions exist and people ship them; they mostly wish they hadn't.

What would actually flip me to Postgres on the client side:

- **Multiple machines writing one database over a network, with no sync layer of your own.** That's a client-server database and SQLite is the wrong tool. But note this also means you aren't local-first.
- **Many concurrent writers inside your own app.** SQLite in WAL mode allows one writer at a time; readers don't block. A UI thread plus a background indexer plus a sync worker all writing means you'll be tuning `busy_timeout` and serializing writes through a single queue. Doable, and a thing you should decide up front.
- **A Postgres-only extension you genuinely need on device.** PostGIS is the real one; SpatiaLite is weaker. Full-text (FTS5) and vector search (sqlite-vec) are covered.

The reason I don't think you can get this "wrong" in the way you're worried about: the engine is the cheapest thing to change later, as long as you keep the SQL boring and don't scatter engine-specific syntax through the app. What's expensive to reverse is the sync design. Deciding between last-write-wins and CRDTs, choosing whether IDs are UUIDs generated on device or sequences from a server, and deciding whether deletes are tombstones or real: those choices propagate into your schema and your on-disk data on users' machines, and changing them later means a migration you have to run on data you can't see. Spend the anxiety there.
