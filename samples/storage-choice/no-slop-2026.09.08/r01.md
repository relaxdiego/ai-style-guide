SQLite, unless "local-first" in your case means a server you run and users connect to.

For an app where data lives on the user's device and works offline, Postgres is the wrong shape: it's a separate server process you'd have to bundle, supervise, port-assign, and version-upgrade on machines you don't control. Embedded Postgres distributions exist and they're roughly 50MB of binaries plus a data directory whose format changes across major versions, meaning a failed upgrade on a user's laptop is your support ticket. SQLite runs in-process, is a single file, ships on macOS/Windows/Linux/iOS/Android/WASM, and every local-first sync stack targets it.

Where Postgres actually wins:

- Multiple OS processes writing concurrently to the same database (SQLite serializes writers; WAL mode gives you one writer plus many readers).
- Types and extensions you'd otherwise reimplement: `jsonb` with indexes, arrays, PostGIS, real full-text search, `pgvector`.
- Datasets past roughly tens of GB with query plans that need a real planner and statistics.
- A shared backend that many clients hit over the network.

The common answer for a syncing app is both: SQLite on the client, Postgres on the server. That's exactly the shape PowerSync and ElectricSQL are built around, and Turso/libSQL if you want SQLite on both ends. The storage engine is also the cheaper half of this decision to reverse. What's expensive to change later is the sync and conflict-resolution model (last-write-wins vs. CRDTs vs. an operation log) and a schema built around it, so if you want to spend your worry budget somewhere, spend it there. Keep your SQL portable and behind one data-access module and swapping engines stays a week of work rather than a rewrite.
