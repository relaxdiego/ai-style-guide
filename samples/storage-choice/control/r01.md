SQLite on the device, and treat the storage engine as the least consequential part of this decision.

For a genuinely local-first app the engine is close to forced. Postgres wants a server process, a data directory, a port, a user account, and a supervision story on every machine your app runs on, and you inherit its crash recovery and version-upgrade behaviour on hardware you can't see. SQLite is already present in iOS, Android, Python, and most desktop runtimes, it's a single file you can copy while the app is closed, and its failure mode when a user force-quits mid-write is well understood by a very large number of people. There are ways to get Postgres semantics locally, either embedded-postgres binaries or PGlite in WebAssembly, and PGlite in particular is a reasonable thing to reach for if you have Postgres-specific SQL you don't want to rewrite. Both cost you install size, startup time, and a platform matrix you'll be maintaining rather than consuming.

The decision that will actually be expensive to get wrong is sync, and in practice your sync choice picks the engine rather than the other way round:

- **CRDT-based** (Automerge, Yjs, cr-sqlite): conflicts resolve automatically, you give up some control over merge semantics, and the document or table model is imposed on your schema.
- **Server-authoritative replication** (ElectricSQL, PowerSync, Turso embedded replicas): SQLite on the client, Postgres on the server, with the sync protocol supplied for you. This constrains what you can do offline more than CRDTs do.
- **Your own operation log**: full control over conflict resolution and the most work, and the option people most often underestimate.
- **No sync at all**, if the data never leaves the machine, in which case none of this matters and SQLite is simply the right answer.

Notice that the second of those isn't an either/or on the original question: the common architecture for a local-first app with a backend is SQLite on the device and Postgres on the server, chosen for different reasons, and asking which one "the app" uses is already the wrong question if you're headed there.

If reversibility is what you're buying, buy it in the schema rather than in the engine. Use `STRICT` tables so SQLite's dynamic typing doesn't quietly let inconsistent data accumulate, store timestamps in one format you've decided on, keep UUIDs in a form Postgres can ingest, and don't build the core of the app on `json_each` or FTS5 without knowing what you'd replace them with. Do that and a later move to Postgres is mechanical work over a weekend. The sync protocol and its conflict semantics are the part you can't redo cheaply once other people's devices are running the old version, because you have to support both sides of the change simultaneously for as long as stragglers exist.

What would flip this is if "local" turns out to mean one machine serving several clients over a network. SQLite's single-writer model and its poor behaviour on network filesystems make that its worst case, and a self-hosted app where the user runs one server for their household or team is Postgres territory, not local-first in the sense above. The same goes for a hard dependency on PostGIS, ranked full-text search, row-level security, or `jsonb` operators you'd otherwise be reimplementing by hand.
