SQLite on the device, and treat Postgres as a separate question about your server that you can answer later.

The device side isn't really a choice once you look at what Postgres is. It's a server process with a data directory, a config file, a version that has to be upgraded in lockstep with its on-disk format, and a supervisor keeping it alive. On a machine you control, that's routine. On a laptop belonging to someone who closes the lid mid-transaction and doesn't know your app has a database, it's an ongoing support burden you'd be signing up for permanently. Embedded Postgres distributions exist and they work, but they don't remove that burden, they just hide it until the first failed `pg_upgrade`. SQLite is a library you link into your process, the database is one file you can copy, and every desktop and mobile platform already ships a copy.

The one thing that would flip this is if "local-first" means your development loop is local but the app is a normal client-server web app. That's a different phrase for a different thing, and there the answer inverts: use Postgres from the start, because you'll deploy on Postgres and the divergence between the two engines is exactly the kind that surfaces in production and not in tests.

Assuming you mean the real local-first case, where the user's data lives on their machine and syncs, the part you should be worried about getting wrong isn't the storage engine. It's the sync model: how you reconcile two devices that edited the same record while offline, whether you're doing last-write-wins per field, CRDTs, or a server-authoritative log that clients replay. That decision leaks into your schema, since CRDTs want you to store per-field metadata and a server-authoritative log wants you to store intents rather than rows. Related, and just as sticky, is schema migration against clients you can't force to upgrade, so version N of your app will be reading databases written by version N-3. Those two choices are genuinely hard to reverse. Swapping SQLite for something else later is a weekend of work by comparison.

The SQLite limitations that actually bite in this setting are worth knowing going in. Its default typing is dynamic and will happily store the string `"seven"` in an integer column, so declare tables `STRICT` and take the type errors at insert time. There's no native date or boolean type, so pick a text or integer convention early and hold it. `ALTER TABLE` is narrow, and anything beyond adding, renaming, or dropping a column means recreating the table and copying data, which is fine but has an ordering you need to follow to avoid mangling foreign keys. And there's one writer at a time, which in WAL mode doesn't block readers and is a non-issue for a single app process, but does mean a background sync worker and your UI thread need to go through one serialized write path rather than contending.

Don't write an abstraction layer to keep both options open. The two engines disagree about types, about upsert syntax, about what `RETURNING` gives you and about how strictly they'll parse a date, and a layer thin enough to be cheap won't hide those, while one thick enough to hide them costs more than a migration would if you ever actually did it. Write straightforward SQLite, keep it behind whatever data-access boundary you'd want anyway for testing, and pay the porting cost only in the world where you need it.

The cases where I'd reconsider are narrow, and none of them are about scale:

- Multiple independent processes writing the same store concurrently, not just multiple threads in your app. SQLite's file locking handles this, but badly under contention, and it's the one place Postgres's MVCC is doing something you can't replicate.
- A hard dependency on a Postgres extension, most commonly pgvector for embeddings or PostGIS for geometry. SQLite has answers for both, `sqlite-vec` and SpatiaLite, and they're decent, but they're not equivalent.
- Analytical queries over hundreds of millions of rows on device, where the answer is probably DuckDB rather than either of these.

For the server side, when you get there, Postgres is the default and there's little to weigh. It's also what most of the off-the-shelf sync engines assume: PowerSync and ElectricSQL both sync a Postgres source down to SQLite clients, and if you'd rather not build the reconciliation logic yourself, adopting one of those settles your server choice as a side effect.
