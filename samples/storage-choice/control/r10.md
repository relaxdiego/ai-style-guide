Use SQLite, and spend the deliberation you were about to spend here on the sync layer instead.

Local-first, in the usual sense, means every client holds a full replica that reads and writes work against while offline. Postgres fits that role badly: it is a server process with a data directory, a port, a role system, and a version-locked on-disk format, so embedding it means shipping and supervising a database server per user, plus a migration story for what happens when a user's laptop is three Postgres majors behind. People do it anyway with embedded-postgres wrappers or PGlite compiled to wasm, but you are paying packaging and operational cost for concurrency and extension features that a single-user replica does not need. SQLite links into your process, keeps one file, needs no administration, and is already present or trivially buildable on every platform you would target, browsers included via wasm and OPFS.

On the worry about getting it wrong: the storage engine is one of the more reversible pieces here. Schemas migrate, and one user's few hundred megabytes can be dumped and reloaded on a background thread. What is not reversible is how you decide what happens when two devices edit the same record while partitioned, because that decision leaks into your schema, your API, and the semantics your UI promises the user. Settle it first and the engine question mostly resolves itself. The broad options, ordered by how much they take off your hands:

- A CRDT document store such as Automerge or Yjs. Merge semantics come for free, but you lose SQL over the merged state unless you maintain a SQLite projection alongside it, which many teams end up doing.
- A sync engine layered on SQLite: ElectricSQL, PowerSync, Turso/libSQL embedded replicas, cr-sqlite. You keep SQL and get replication, at the cost of a dependency with strong opinions about your schema.
- Hand-rolled sync with per-row version columns, a change-log table, and last-write-wins or a domain-specific merge. Workable for append-mostly or single-writer-per-record data, genuinely hard for collaborative editing.

The middle option usually lands you on both engines: SQLite on the device, Postgres as the canonical server store. So the choice is often not exclusive, and Postgres reappears on its own terms rather than as a client-side compromise.

Postgres does win outright if "local-first" for you means a self-hosted server running on the user's own machine rather than an offline-capable client. Concurrent writers across processes, PostGIS, full-text search with real ranking configuration, jsonb indexing, logical replication as the sync primitive itself: SQLite has no answer to those. If what you are building is something a small team runs from a compose file on a NAS, that is the Postgres case and I would not fight it.

Assuming SQLite, four things are much cheaper to adopt on day one than to retrofit. Turn on WAL mode, since SQLite allows one writer at a time and WAL is what keeps readers from blocking behind it. Declare tables `STRICT`, because the default type affinity will otherwise let a string into an integer column and you will find out months later. Expect migrations to mean table rebuilds, as `ALTER TABLE` supports little beyond adding and renaming columns. And bundle your own SQLite build instead of linking the system one, because the version shipped on older Android and iOS predates `RETURNING`, strict tables, and much of the JSON surface you will want.
