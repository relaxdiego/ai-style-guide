TCP congestion control is a feedback loop: the sender keeps a congestion window (`cwnd`) limiting how much unacknowledged data may be in flight, grows it while ACKs keep arriving, and shrinks it when it sees a congestion signal. Actual send limit is `min(cwnd, receiver's advertised window)`. Nobody tells the sender the network's capacity, so it probes for it continuously.

The classic state machine (Reno, RFC 5681) has four parts:

- **Slow start.** `cwnd` starts around 10 MSS (RFC 6928) and increases by 1 MSS per ACK, so it doubles every RTT. Exponential probing to find the rough capacity fast. Ends at `ssthresh` or at the first loss.
- **Congestion avoidance.** `cwnd` increases by roughly 1 MSS per RTT, linear. This is the steady-state probing mode.
- **Fast retransmit / fast recovery.** Three duplicate ACKs mean a packet was lost but the pipe is still flowing. Retransmit immediately, set `ssthresh = cwnd/2`, set `cwnd` to the new `ssthresh`, stay in congestion avoidance.
- **Retransmission timeout.** No ACKs at all. Assume the worst: `cwnd` drops to 1 MSS and slow start restarts. Far more expensive than fast recovery, which is why SACK and RACK exist to keep losses in the fast-recovery path.

Linear increase with multiplicative decrease (AIMD) is what makes competing flows converge on a fair share: on each loss event every flow cuts by the same *proportion*, which shrinks the gap between a greedy flow and a small one, while the additive increase adds the same absolute amount to each. The consequence is the well-known throughput relation, roughly `MSS / (RTT · √p)` for loss rate `p`, which says a flow's share falls with its RTT and that a single loss-based flow cannot fill a high bandwidth-delay-product link. That limitation drove the modern variants:

| Algorithm | Congestion signal | Behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| Reno / NewReno | Packet loss | AIMD, halve on loss. The baseline. |
| CUBIC (Linux, Windows default) | Packet loss | `cwnd` follows a cubic curve of time since the last reduction: fast recovery back toward the previous maximum, then cautious probing above it. Cuts by 0.7 instead of 0.5, and growth does not depend on RTT, so it scales to fast, long links and is fairer across mixed RTTs. |
| BBR (Google, QUIC) | Measured bottleneck bandwidth and minimum RTT | Ignores loss as a signal. Estimates delivery rate, paces sending at that rate, periodically probes up and drains. Keeps queues short, so it avoids bufferbloat, but coexistence with CUBIC is uneven. |
| DCTCP | ECN marking depth | Datacenter only. Uses the *fraction* of marked packets to scale the cut proportionally rather than halving. Needs switch support. |
| Vegas | Rising RTT | Delay-based, reacts before loss, gets starved by loss-based flows sharing a queue. |

Two practical points. Loss-based control only backs off once a buffer somewhere has already filled, so on links with oversized buffers it drives standing queueing delay into the hundreds of milliseconds; the fix is at the router, with active queue management such as FQ-CoDel, not at the sender. And ECN lets a router mark a packet instead of dropping it, giving the sender the same signal without the retransmission, which is why it is standard inside datacenters and increasingly enabled on the public internet.
