TCP congestion control is a feedback loop in the sender: it keeps a congestion window, allows no more than that many unacknowledged bytes on the wire, grows the window while the network looks healthy, and shrinks it hard when it sees a congestion signal. Bytes in flight are capped at `min(cwnd, rwnd)`, where `rwnd` is the receiver's advertised window and covers a different problem (a slow receiver, not a busy network). Classic TCP gets no help from routers, so it infers congestion from packet loss, detected either by duplicate ACKs or by a retransmission timeout.

## The four classic phases (Tahoe/Reno)

- **Slow start.** `cwnd` begins at the initial window, 10 MSS on most modern stacks, and increases by 1 MSS per ACK, so it doubles every RTT. Exponential, despite the name; the "slow" is relative to the older behaviour of blasting a full receiver window immediately. It runs until `cwnd` reaches `ssthresh` or a loss occurs.
- **Congestion avoidance.** Above `ssthresh`, `cwnd` grows by roughly 1 MSS per RTT (implemented as `cwnd += MSS*MSS/cwnd` per ACK). This is the linear probe for more bandwidth.
- **Fast retransmit and fast recovery.** Three duplicate ACKs mean a packet was lost but later packets arrived, so the path still works. Reno retransmits immediately, sets `ssthresh = cwnd/2`, sets `cwnd` to the new `ssthresh`, and continues in congestion avoidance rather than restarting.
- **Timeout.** No ACK at all within the retransmission timeout is treated as severe congestion: `ssthresh = cwnd/2`, `cwnd = 1 MSS`, back to slow start.

The additive-increase/multiplicative-decrease rule in the middle two phases is what makes competing flows converge toward a fair share. Chiu and Jain's argument: additive increase moves two flows' rates along a 45-degree line toward equality, multiplicative decrease moves them along a line toward the origin preserving their ratio, and repeating the two drives the ratio toward 1:1. Increase additively and decrease multiplicatively, or the system either fails to converge or oscillates without ever backing off enough.

## Why Reno was replaced

Reno's steady-state throughput is about `(MSS/RTT) · 1.22/√p` for loss rate `p`. Filling a 10 Gbps path with 1500-byte packets and 100 ms RTT needs roughly 83,000 packets in flight, which that formula reaches only at a loss rate near 2·10⁻¹⁰, well below the error rate of real links. Long fat pipes also recover too slowly: after a loss, adding 1 MSS per RTT takes over an hour to climb back.

| Algorithm | Congestion signal | Window/rate rule |
|---|---|---|
| Reno | Loss (3 dup ACKs or timeout) | AIMD, multiply by 0.5 on loss |
| CUBIC (Linux default) | Loss | `W(t) = C(t−K)³ + W_max`, C=0.4, multiply by 0.7 on loss; growth depends on time since last loss, not RTT, so it is fairer across RTTs and fast on big pipes |
| Vegas | Queueing delay (RTT above minimum) | Adjust to hold a small constant number of packets queued; backs off before loss, so it loses to loss-based flows sharing a buffer |
| BBR | Estimated bottleneck bandwidth and minimum RTT | Paces at `gain × BtlBw`, `cwnd ≈ 2 × BDP`; periodically probes up for bandwidth and drains down to re-measure RTT. Ignores loss as a primary signal (v2/v3 add loss and ECN response) |

## Signals beyond loss

Loss as the only signal has a cost: the sender only learns about congestion after a router's buffer has already filled, so deep buffers produce hundreds of milliseconds of standing queue (bufferbloat) while throughput stays fine. Two fixes address it from opposite ends. ECN lets a router set a mark in the IP header instead of dropping; the receiver echoes it, and the sender reduces its window as if it had seen a loss, with no retransmission needed. Active queue management at the router (CoDel, FQ-CoDel, PIE) drops or marks based on how long packets sit in the queue rather than on queue occupancy, which keeps latency bounded regardless of buffer size. Paced sending, now standard in Linux and mandatory in BBR and QUIC, spreads a window over an RTT instead of releasing it as a burst, which prevents a sender from creating the very queue it is trying to avoid.

Everything above is sender-side and per-connection, which is why it can be changed unilaterally (`net.ipv4.tcp_congestion_control` on Linux, or a library setting in QUIC, where the same algorithms run in userspace over UDP).
