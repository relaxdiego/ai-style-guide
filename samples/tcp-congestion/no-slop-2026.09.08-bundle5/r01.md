TCP congestion control is a sender-side feedback loop: the sender guesses how much unacknowledged data the path can hold, sends that much, and revises the guess based on what comes back.

The state is a congestion window (cwnd), a byte count of data allowed in flight at once. What the sender may actually have outstanding is `min(cwnd, receiver's advertised window)`, which keeps flow control (protect the receiver's buffer) and congestion control (protect the network) as separate limits on the same quantity. Routers normally tell the sender nothing, so the sender infers capacity from the ACK stream: each ACK means one packet has left the network, so another may enter, and a missing ACK probably means a router queue filled up and dropped it. Everything below is built on that inference.

Classic loss-based control (Reno) moves cwnd through four states:

- **Slow start.** cwnd begins at about 10 MSS and doubles every round trip (one MSS added per ACK). The growth is exponential; "slow" is relative to the pre-1988 behaviour of sending a full receiver window immediately. Continues until loss, or until cwnd reaches the slow-start threshold `ssthresh`.
- **Congestion avoidance.** cwnd grows by roughly one MSS per round trip, probing linearly for spare capacity.
- **Fast retransmit and fast recovery.** Three duplicate ACKs mean one segment is missing while later ones are still arriving, so the path is alive. Resend that segment at once, set ssthresh and cwnd to half the current window, and resume congestion avoidance rather than restarting.
- **Retransmission timeout.** Nothing came back at all. Assume the pipe is empty, drop cwnd to 1 MSS, re-enter slow start, and double the timeout for each further failure.

Halving on loss and adding a constant per RTT is what makes this converge. Chiu and Jain showed in 1989 that among linear control rules, additive increase with multiplicative decrease is the combination that drives competing flows on a shared bottleneck toward equal shares from any starting point. Fairness is per connection, not per host or per user, which is why opening eight parallel connections gets you eight shares, and why flows with short round trips beat long ones: they run the increase loop more often per second.

Reno's linear increase is too slow for modern high bandwidth-delay paths, so the deployed algorithms differ mainly in what they treat as the congestion signal:

| Algorithm | Reacts to | Window growth | Where |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reno / NewReno | Packet loss | Linear, +1 MSS/RTT | Reference, legacy |
| CUBIC | Packet loss | Cubic function of time since last loss: fast recovery toward the previous window, cautious probing above it | Linux and Windows default |
| BBR | Measured bottleneck bandwidth and minimum RTT | Paces at estimated bandwidth, periodically probes up and drains | Google, YouTube, QUIC deployments |
| DCTCP | ECN marks, proportional to how many | Decrease scaled to the marking rate, not a flat halve | Datacentres with ECN-capable switches |

The reason for signals other than loss is that loss-based control only backs off once a queue is completely full, so it deliberately drives buffers to saturation. On paths with large unmanaged router buffers this adds hundreds of milliseconds of standing queue delay for every flow sharing the link. Two fixes exist and they compose: change the sender to react before the queue fills (BBR estimates bandwidth and minimum RTT directly, DCTCP reacts to Explicit Congestion Notification marks that a switch sets at a shallow threshold rather than dropping), or change the router to keep the queue short with an active queue management scheme like CoDel or FQ-CoDel. Modern senders also pace packets out at the window's implied rate instead of releasing them in a burst on each ACK, which matters because a burst can overflow a buffer that the average rate would never have troubled.
