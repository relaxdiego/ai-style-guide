TCP congestion control is a feedback loop in which the sender guesses how much data the path can hold, sends that much, and revises the guess from what comes back. The network never announces its capacity, so the sender probes: raise the rate until something breaks, back off, repeat.

The whole state is one number, the congestion window (`cwnd`). The sender may have at most `min(cwnd, receiver_window)` bytes unacknowledged in flight, so throughput is roughly `cwnd / RTT`. Each arriving ACK does double duty as a delivery receipt and as permission to send more, which is why a TCP sender's transmissions are naturally paced at the bottleneck rate once the pipe is full.

**The four phases (Reno/NewReno, still the model everything else is described against)**

- **Slow start.** `cwnd` begins around 10 MSS and grows by 1 MSS per ACK, so it doubles every RTT. The growth is exponential despite the name; it is "slow" only relative to blasting a full receiver window immediately. Ends at `ssthresh` or at the first loss.
- **Congestion avoidance.** `cwnd` grows by about 1 MSS per RTT (`cwnd += MSS*MSS/cwnd` per ACK). Linear probing for newly available capacity.
- **Fast retransmit and fast recovery.** Three duplicate ACKs, or SACK blocks showing a hole, mean a packet was lost but later ones arrived, so the path still works. Retransmit the missing segment, set `ssthresh = cwnd/2`, drop `cwnd` to that, resume congestion avoidance.
- **Retransmission timeout.** No ACK before the RTO fires. This is the pessimistic case: `cwnd` collapses to 1 MSS and slow start begins again, with the RTO doubled on each successive failure.

The increase/decrease pairing is the point. Chiu and Jain showed that additive increase with multiplicative decrease converges to an equal split of a shared bottleneck regardless of starting conditions, while the other three combinations either fail to converge or converge to unfairness.

Two problems drove everything since. First, a loss-based controller only stops growing when a buffer overflows, so it holds deep router queues full and inflates latency for everyone crossing that link (bufferbloat). Second, Reno's steady-state throughput is about `MSS / (RTT * sqrt(p))` for loss rate `p`, so a 10 Gbps path at 100 ms RTT needs a packet loss rate below roughly 10⁻¹⁰ to stay saturated, which no real link provides.

| Algorithm | Signal it reacts to | Behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| Reno/NewReno | Packet loss | AIMD, halve on loss. The reference, rarely the default now. |
| CUBIC | Packet loss | Linux default since 2.6.19. `cwnd` follows a cubic curve in time since the last loss: fast recovery toward the previous peak, then cautious probing above it. Growth depends on elapsed time rather than ACK arrivals, so it does not starve long-RTT flows. Reduces by 30% rather than 50%. |
| BBR | Bottleneck bandwidth and minimum RTT | Ignores loss as a congestion signal. Estimates delivery rate and `min_RTT`, then paces at that bandwidth with roughly one BDP in flight. Keeps queues short; can be aggressive against CUBIC on shallow buffers. |
| DCTCP | ECN marks | Datacenter use with ECN-marking switches. Reduces `cwnd` in proportion to the fraction of marked packets, so it can hold very shallow queues. Needs a controlled network. |
| Vegas | Rising RTT | Backs off on queue buildup before loss. Loses badly when competing with loss-based flows, which is why it stayed academic. |

The router side matters as much as the sender. Active queue management (CoDel, FQ-CoDel, PIE) drops or ECN-marks packets before the buffer fills, giving the sender a congestion signal early enough that the queue never grows deep, and fair queueing isolates a bulk transfer from an interactive flow so the two do not share one FIFO. Sender-side pacing, spreading a window's packets over an RTT instead of sending them back to back, has also become standard, since ACK clocking alone produces bursts that overflow small buffers.
