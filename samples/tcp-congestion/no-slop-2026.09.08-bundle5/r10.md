TCP congestion control is a feedback loop: the sender guesses how much data the network path can hold, sends that much, watches for signs of overload, and adjusts. The guess lives in a sender-side variable called the congestion window (cwnd). At any moment the sender may have at most `min(cwnd, receiver_window)` bytes unacknowledged in flight. The receiver window protects the receiver's buffer; cwnd protects the network. Nothing in the protocol tells the sender the path's capacity, so cwnd is derived entirely from observing what happens to packets already sent.

**The classic control loop (Reno/NewReno)**

- **Slow start.** Begin with cwnd around 10 MSS. Increase cwnd by one MSS per ACK received, which doubles it every round trip. Exponential probing, despite the name. It continues until cwnd reaches a threshold called ssthresh or until a loss occurs.
- **Congestion avoidance.** Above ssthresh, increase cwnd by roughly one MSS per round trip instead of per ACK. Linear growth, on the assumption the path is now near capacity.
- **Fast retransmit.** Three duplicate ACKs for the same sequence number mean a packet was probably lost while later ones arrived. Resend it immediately rather than waiting for the retransmission timer.
- **Fast recovery.** After that loss, set ssthresh to half of cwnd, set cwnd to the new ssthresh, and resume congestion avoidance. The connection halves its rate instead of collapsing to one packet.
- **Timeout.** If the retransmission timer (RTO) fires, the sender assumes something much worse: it drops cwnd to one MSS and re-enters slow start. This is the expensive path, and modern stacks work hard to avoid it using SACK and RACK-TLP.

That increase-by-one, halve-on-loss pattern is additive increase, multiplicative decrease. Chiu and Jain showed in 1989 that among flows sharing a bottleneck and seeing the same loss signal, AIMD converges toward an equal split, which is why the shape survived. It also has a known weakness: throughput scales roughly as `MSS / (RTT * sqrt(p))` for loss probability p, so a flow with a long round trip loses badly to a short-RTT flow at the same bottleneck, and a 10 Gb/s path with a 100 ms RTT needs a loss rate near 10⁻¹⁰ for Reno to fill it.

**What actually runs today**

| Algorithm | Congestion signal | Increase rule | Where it is used |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reno / NewReno | Packet loss | Linear per RTT, halve on loss | Reference, legacy stacks |
| CUBIC | Packet loss | Cubic function of time since last loss, cut to 70% | Linux and Windows default |
| BBR (v1–v3) | Measured bandwidth and minimum RTT | Send at estimated bottleneck rate, probe periodically | Google, YouTube, many CDNs |
| DCTCP | ECN mark fraction | Reduce proportional to marking rate | Data centres with ECN-capable switches |
| Vegas / Copa | Rising queueing delay | Adjust to hold a small standing queue | Research, some hybrids |

CUBIC decouples growth from RTT by making cwnd a function of elapsed wall-clock time since the last reduction, so long-RTT flows are not punished as severely, and it spends most of its time in a plateau near the last known good window before probing higher. BBR takes a different position: it treats loss as a poor proxy for congestion, since routers with deep buffers drop nothing while adding hundreds of milliseconds of delay (bufferbloat). BBR estimates the bottleneck bandwidth and the minimum round-trip time, then paces packets at that rate, keeping the buffer nearly empty. The tradeoff is that BBR and loss-based flows sharing a queue do not necessarily split it evenly, and BBRv1 was aggressive enough against CUBIC that v2 and v3 exist largely to fix this.

Two practical points that trip people up. First, congestion control is separate from flow control; a connection can be limited by the receiver's advertised window and never involve cwnd at all, which is a common cause of "slow transfer" reports that have nothing to do with the network. Second, explicit congestion notification (ECN) lets a router mark a packet instead of dropping it, giving the sender the same signal without the retransmission, but it requires support at both endpoints and in the bottleneck router, so its deployment is uneven outside data centres.
