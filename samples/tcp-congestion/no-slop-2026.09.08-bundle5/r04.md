TCP congestion control is a sender-side feedback loop that guesses how much unacknowledged data the path can hold. The sender keeps a congestion window (cwnd) and limits bytes in flight to `min(cwnd, receiver's advertised window)`. No router tells it the right value, so it raises cwnd until the network signals overload, then cuts it. The classic signal is packet loss, inferred from duplicate ACKs or a retransmission timeout.

Reno, the reference behavior everything else is measured against, has four states:

- **Slow start.** cwnd starts at about 10 MSS on Linux and doubles every round trip (one MSS added per ACK). Exponential, despite the name, and it runs until cwnd passes `ssthresh` or a loss happens.
- **Congestion avoidance.** cwnd grows by roughly one MSS per round trip. Additive increase, probing gently for more capacity.
- **Fast retransmit and fast recovery.** Three duplicate ACKs mean a packet was lost but later ones arrived, so the path still works. The sender resends the missing segment, halves cwnd and ssthresh, and continues from there instead of restarting.
- **Timeout.** No ACK at all within the retransmission timeout means the sender learned nothing about the path. cwnd drops to 1 MSS and slow start begins again. This is the expensive case, and the RTO is backed off exponentially on repeats.

The additive increase with multiplicative decrease is what makes competing flows converge on an equal share: increases add the same absolute amount to every flow while decreases take a proportion, so the flow with the larger window gives up more. The cost is that steady-state throughput for Reno is about `MSS / (RTT × √p)` for loss rate `p`, which collapses on long fat paths. A 10 Gbit/s transfer at 100 ms RTT needs a loss rate below roughly 10⁻¹⁰ to stay at rate, and recovery after one loss takes over an hour.

That formula is why Linux defaults to CUBIC. CUBIC grows cwnd as a cubic function of the time since the last reduction, flattening near the previous window and then climbing aggressively past it, and it uses time rather than ACK arrivals so short-RTT flows do not automatically dominate. It also cuts to 0.7 of the window instead of 0.5. Loss-based algorithms share one defect: they only back off after filling the bottleneck queue, so they drive up latency for everyone on that link. Two responses to that are in wide use. ECN lets a router mark a packet instead of dropping it, and the sender treats the mark as it would a loss. BBR ignores loss and estimates the bottleneck bandwidth and minimum RTT directly, pacing at the product of the two and periodically probing both, which keeps queues short but makes its fairness against CUBIC dependent on buffer size.
