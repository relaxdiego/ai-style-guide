TCP congestion control limits how much unacknowledged data a sender puts in flight, so senders collectively don't overrun the slowest link or the buffers in front of it. The sender maintains a congestion window (cwnd) in bytes; the actual send limit is `min(cwnd, receiver's advertised window)`. The receiver window protects the receiver's buffer; cwnd protects the network. There is no signal from routers telling you the right rate (mostly), so TCP discovers it by increasing until something breaks, then backing off.

## The control loop

ACKs are the clock. Each ACK says one packet left the network, which permits roughly one new packet in. That self-clocking alone keeps the send rate near the bottleneck rate once the pipe is full; cwnd adjustments just steer it.

The classic algorithm has four parts:

- **Slow start.** Begin at an initial window (10 MSS in current practice, RFC 6928). Increase cwnd by 1 MSS per ACK, which doubles cwnd every round trip. Exponential, despite the name. Runs until loss or until cwnd reaches `ssthresh`.
- **Congestion avoidance.** Above `ssthresh`, increase cwnd by about 1 MSS per round trip instead of doubling. Linear probing for more bandwidth.
- **Fast retransmit.** Three duplicate ACKs mean a packet is probably lost while later packets arrived. Retransmit immediately rather than waiting for the retransmission timeout.
- **Fast recovery.** After fast retransmit, halve `ssthresh` and cwnd and continue in congestion avoidance, rather than collapsing to 1 MSS.

A retransmission timeout is the pessimistic path: it means no ACKs are arriving at all, so the sender assumes the pipe is empty, sets cwnd back to the initial window, and re-enters slow start.

## Why additive increase, multiplicative decrease

AIMD is the part that makes independent senders converge. Adding a constant per RTT and multiplying by a constant (0.5 in Reno, 0.7 in CUBIC) on loss drives competing flows toward an equal share, and does so stably. Additive-increase/additive-decrease or multiplicative-increase variants either don't converge to fairness or oscillate. The cost is throughput proportional to `1/(RTT * sqrt(p))` for loss rate `p`, which is why Reno cannot fill a 10 Gbps transcontinental link: the required loss rate is absurdly low, and each loss cuts the window in half.

## What modern stacks actually run

| Algorithm | Signal | Behavior |
|---|---|---|
| Reno / NewReno | Packet loss | AIMD, halve on loss. The baseline. |
| CUBIC | Packet loss | Window grows as a cubic function of time since the last loss: fast recovery back to the previous window, then cautious probing above it. RTT-independent growth. Linux default. |
| BBR | Bandwidth and RTT estimates | Measures the bottleneck bandwidth and minimum RTT, paces sending at that rate. Ignores loss as a congestion signal. Used heavily by Google. |
| Vegas | Queueing delay | Backs off when RTT rises above baseline. Loses badly when competing with loss-based flows. |
| DCTCP | ECN marks | Reacts proportionally to the fraction of marked packets. Datacenter only, needs switch support. |

## Two problems worth understanding

**Loss as a signal is late and ambiguous.** By the time a router drops a packet, its buffer is already full, so every flow through it is paying a full buffer's worth of delay. Oversized buffers make this much worse: a loss-based sender fills them, adding hundreds of milliseconds of latency without adding throughput. This is bufferbloat. Fixes come from the queue side (fq_codel, CAKE) rather than the sender.

Loss is also ambiguous on wireless links, where packets are lost to radio interference rather than congestion, and TCP backs off for no reason.

**ECN gives you the signal earlier.** Routers mark packets instead of dropping them when a queue starts building; the receiver echoes the mark, and the sender reduces its window without losing data. It works well when deployed, and deployment has been slow because middleboxes historically mangled the bits.

BBR attacks the first problem by modeling the path instead of waiting for drops. It has its own fairness arguments against CUBIC, particularly with shallow buffers, and BBRv2/v3 exist largely to address those.
