TCP congestion control is a feedback loop where the sender guesses how much unacknowledged data the network can hold, and adjusts that guess based on whether packets arrive or get lost. The network never tells the sender its capacity, so the sender probes upward until something breaks, then backs off.

## The control variable

The sender maintains a congestion window (`cwnd`), in bytes. It may have at most `min(cwnd, receiver's advertised window)` bytes in flight (sent but unacknowledged). Throughput is roughly `cwnd / RTT`. Everything below is a rule for moving `cwnd`.

`cwnd` is sender-side state that no one else sees. The receiver's advertised window handles a different problem: a slow receiver. Congestion control handles a slow network.

## The classic four algorithms (Reno, RFC 5681)

**Slow start.** Start `cwnd` at about 10 MSS. Increase by 1 MSS per ACK, which doubles `cwnd` every RTT. Exponential, despite the name. Exit when `cwnd` exceeds `ssthresh` (slow start threshold) or when loss occurs.

**Congestion avoidance.** Once past `ssthresh`, increase `cwnd` by roughly 1 MSS per RTT instead of doubling. Linear probing for extra capacity.

**Fast retransmit.** Three duplicate ACKs mean a segment is missing but later ones arrived, so the path still works. Retransmit immediately rather than waiting for a timeout.

**Fast recovery.** After fast retransmit, halve `cwnd` and `ssthresh` and continue in congestion avoidance instead of restarting from scratch. A retransmission timeout (RTO), by contrast, is treated as severe: `cwnd` drops to 1 MSS and slow start restarts.

Together the steady-state behavior is AIMD: additive increase, multiplicative decrease. AIMD is what makes competing flows converge on a fair share. Increasing additively and decreasing multiplicatively pushes flows toward equality; the reverse would not.

The cost is the Reno throughput model: about `MSS / (RTT · √p)` for loss probability `p`. On a 10 Gbit/s link with 100 ms RTT, Reno needs a loss rate near 10⁻¹⁰ to keep the pipe full, and takes over an hour to recover from a single loss. That is why nobody runs Reno on fast long paths.

## What actually runs today

| Algorithm | Signal | Where it is used |
|---|---|---|
| CUBIC | Loss | Linux and Windows default |
| BBR (v1/v2/v3) | Bandwidth and RTT estimates, plus loss and ECN in v2+ | Google services, QUIC, widely deployed |
| DCTCP | ECN marks | Datacenter fabrics with ECN-capable switches |
| Vegas | Queueing delay | Mostly research; loses badly against loss-based flows |

**CUBIC** replaces the linear increase with a cubic function of time since the last congestion event. It jumps back near the previous window quickly, then flattens out around it (the plateau where capacity was last known good), then probes aggressively past it. Growth depends on elapsed time rather than ACK arrivals, so short-RTT and long-RTT flows compete more evenly than under Reno. Its decrease factor is 0.7, gentler than Reno's 0.5.

**BBR** rejects loss as the primary signal. It estimates bottleneck bandwidth (max delivery rate observed) and minimum round-trip propagation delay, then sends at that bandwidth with an amount in flight near the bandwidth-delay product. It paces packets at the estimated rate instead of dumping a window into the network, and periodically probes 25% above its estimate to find new capacity, then drains. BBRv1 was unfair to CUBIC flows in deep buffers and starved flows in shallow ones; v2 and v3 add loss and ECN response to fix that.

## Why the signal choice matters: bufferbloat

Loss-based algorithms fill the bottleneck queue by definition. They only back off when a router drops a packet, which happens only when its buffer is full. Router buffers are often sized in hundreds of milliseconds, so a single bulk transfer adds that much latency to every other flow sharing the link. Your video call degrades because a backup is running.

Two fixes:

- **Router side:** active queue management. CoDel and FQ-CoDel drop or mark packets based on how long they sat in the queue, giving the sender an early signal and keeping the queue short. FQ-CoDel also isolates flows so a bulk transfer cannot delay a sparse one.
- **Endpoint side:** delay or rate signals instead of loss (Vegas, BBR), or explicit marks (ECN, DCTCP, L4S).

**ECN** lets a router set a bit in the IP header instead of dropping the packet. The receiver echoes it back, the sender reacts as if to loss. Congestion feedback without data loss. Classic ECN treats a mark exactly like a drop; DCTCP and L4S instead react proportionally to the *fraction* of packets marked, which gives much finer control and keeps queues near-empty.

## The parts people forget

- **Pacing.** Without it, an arriving ACK burst causes a matching send burst that overflows small buffers regardless of window size. Linux paces by default; BBR requires it.
- **Loss is not always congestion.** On wireless links, corruption looks identical to congestion at the TCP layer. This is a real weakness of loss-based control, mitigated in practice by link-layer retransmission below IP.
- **Congestion control is per-connection.** Opening six connections gets roughly six shares. This is why HTTP/1.1 sharding was effective and why HTTP/2 multiplexing over one connection was a throughput regression on lossy paths.
- **It is sender-side and changeable unilaterally.** You can switch to BBR on your servers today (`net.ipv4.tcp_congestion_control`) without touching clients or routers. Both endpoints and the network keep working; only your sending behavior changes.
- **QUIC does all of this in userspace**, with the same algorithms but better loss detection: unique packet numbers remove the retransmission ambiguity that plagues TCP's RTT estimation.
