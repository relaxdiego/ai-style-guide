TCP congestion control limits how much unacknowledged data a sender puts in flight, so senders collectively don't overload routers between them. The sender keeps a congestion window (cwnd) alongside the receiver's advertised window; it may have at most `min(cwnd, rwnd)` bytes outstanding. Everything else is rules for growing and shrinking cwnd based on inferred network state.

## The core loop

Each ACK is both a permission slip and a measurement. Data leaves the sender at roughly the rate ACKs return, which is roughly the rate the bottleneck link drains its queue. That self-clocking is the base mechanism; cwnd adjustment sits on top of it.

## Phases in classic TCP (Reno/NewReno)

- **Slow start.** cwnd begins at ~10 MSS and doubles every RTT (increase by 1 MSS per ACK). Exponential probing to find the rough scale of available capacity fast. Ends when cwnd crosses `ssthresh` or a loss occurs.
- **Congestion avoidance.** cwnd grows by about 1 MSS per RTT (increase by `MSS*MSS/cwnd` per ACK). Linear probing for slack that may have appeared.
- **Fast retransmit.** Three duplicate ACKs mean a segment is missing but later ones arrived, so the path still works. Retransmit immediately instead of waiting for the retransmission timeout.
- **Fast recovery.** Halve ssthresh to cwnd/2, set cwnd to that, and continue rather than restarting from 1.
- **Timeout.** No ACK at all: assume severe congestion. ssthresh = cwnd/2, cwnd = 1 MSS, back to slow start, and double the RTO for each successive timeout.

That additive-increase / multiplicative-decrease (AIMD) rule is what makes competing flows converge to a fair share: increases are equal in absolute terms, decreases are proportional, so a flow with more bandwidth gives up more on each loss.

## Why loss-based control has aged badly

Reno's throughput scales as roughly `MSS / (RTT * sqrt(p))` for loss probability p. On a 10 Gb/s link with 100 ms RTT you need a loss rate around 10⁻¹⁰ to fill the pipe, which is unreachable. Two responses:

| Algorithm | Signal | Behavior |
|---|---|---|
| CUBIC (Linux default) | Loss | cwnd follows a cubic curve in time since the last loss: fast recovery toward the previous maximum, then a plateau there, then aggressive probing above it. Growth is independent of RTT, so it is fairer across mixed-RTT flows and scales to fat pipes. |
| BBR | Measured bottleneck bandwidth and minimum RTT | Estimates delivery rate and min RTT, sets the sending rate to their product, and periodically probes up and down. Ignores loss as a primary signal, so it works on lossy paths and avoids filling deep buffers. |
| Vegas and similar | RTT increase | Backs off when queuing delay grows, before loss. Loses badly when sharing a bottleneck with loss-based flows, which keep pushing until the queue overflows. |
| DCTCP, ECN | Explicit router marking | Routers set a bit (CE) instead of dropping; the sender reduces in proportion to the fraction of marked packets. Needs router support; standard in datacenters. |

## Two things that trip people up

Deep router buffers turn congestion into latency rather than loss, so loss-based algorithms fill those buffers and add hundreds of milliseconds of delay (bufferbloat). AQM schemes like CoDel and FQ-CoDel fix this at the router by dropping or marking based on how long packets sit in the queue, not on queue length.

Also, congestion control and flow control are separate limits that happen to combine in the same `min()`. Flow control (rwnd) protects the receiver's buffer; congestion control (cwnd) protects the network. A stalled application on the receiver shrinks rwnd and has nothing to do with congestion.
