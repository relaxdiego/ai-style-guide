TCP congestion control is a feedback loop: the sender guesses how much unacknowledged data the network path can hold, sends that much, and adjusts the guess based on whether packets get through.

## The core variable

The sender keeps a congestion window (`cwnd`) in bytes. It may have at most `min(cwnd, receiver_window)` bytes in flight. The receiver window prevents overrunning the receiver's buffer; `cwnd` prevents overrunning the routers in between. Nobody tells the sender the right value for `cwnd`, so it probes for it continuously.

Throughput is roughly `cwnd / RTT`. That relationship drives most of the behavior below.

## The classic four phases (Reno/NewToRenoo lineage)

**Slow start.** Begin with `cwnd` around 10 MSS. Increase by 1 MSS per ACK, which doubles `cwnd` every RTT. Exponential growth, despite the name: it's slow compared to the old behavior of blasting the whole receiver window immediately. Continues until loss occurs or `cwnd` crosses the slow start threshold (`ssthresh`).

**Congestion avoidance.** Above `ssthresh`, increase `cwnd` by about 1 MSS per RTT instead of doubling. Linear growth, gently probing for more capacity.

**Fast retransmit.** Three duplicate ACKs for the same sequence number mean a packet was probably lost while later ones arrived. Retransmit immediately rather than waiting for the retransmission timer.

**Fast recovery.** On that loss signal, halve `cwnd` and continue in congestion avoidance rather than dropping to 1 MSS. A retransmission timeout, which suggests a much worse problem, does collapse `cwnd` to 1 MSS and restarts slow start.

Together the steady-state pattern is AIMD: additive increase, multiplicative decrease. Add 1 per RTT, multiply by 0.5 on loss. AIMD is what makes competing flows converge toward an equal share; a flow using more bandwidth takes a proportionally larger absolute cut on each loss event.

## Why loss is a bad signal

Loss-based control fills the bottleneck buffer before it backs off, because a queue must overflow before the sender learns anything. With today's oversized router buffers, that means hundreds of milliseconds of standing queue delay for everyone sharing the link. Loss also conflates congestion with wireless corruption, which penalizes lossy links unfairly.

Two responses to that:

- **ECN**: routers mark packets instead of dropping them when queues build. The receiver echoes the mark, and the sender reacts as if to loss but without the retransmission. DCTCP extends this by reacting proportionally to the fraction of marked packets, keeping queues short in datacenters.
- **Delay- and rate-based control**: infer congestion from rising RTT or from measured delivery rate instead of drops.

## The algorithms you'll actually meet

| Algorithm | Signal | Where |
|---|---|---|
| Reno / NewReno | Loss | Historic baseline, still the reference |
| CUBIC | Loss | Linux and Windows default |
| BBR | Delivery rate and min RTT | Google services, YouTube, widely deployed |
| DCTCP | ECN marks | Datacenter fabrics |
| Vegas | RTT increase | Rare in practice, loses to loss-based flows |

**CUBIC** grows `cwnd` as a cubic function of time since the last loss. The curve flattens near the window size that caused the previous loss, lingers there to test whether capacity is unchanged, then accelerates past it to probe for more. Growth depends on elapsed time rather than ACK arrivals, so high-latency flows are not punished the way Reno's per-RTT increment punishes them.

**BBR** ignores loss almost entirely. It estimates the bottleneck bandwidth (max delivery rate observed) and the minimum RTT (propagation delay with empty queues), then paces sending at bandwidth × minRTT. It periodically probes upward and downward to refresh both estimates. The result is high throughput with a mostly empty bottleneck queue. Its fairness against CUBIC is mixed and version-dependent; BBRv1 could starve loss-based flows on deep-buffered links, and v2/v3 add ECN and loss responsiveness to fix that.

## Supporting pieces

- **SACK** tells the sender exactly which segments arrived, so one RTT of loss doesn't stall recovery for multiple round trips.
- **Pacing** spreads a window's packets evenly across an RTT instead of sending a burst. Required for BBR, beneficial everywhere.
- **RACK-TLP** uses time-based reordering detection instead of the duplicate-ACK count, which handles reordering and tail losses better.

## What to take away

Every one of these algorithms is answering the same question with different evidence: how much can I have in flight without building a queue? Loss-based algorithms answer it after the fact and pay in delay. Rate- and mark-based algorithms answer it earlier and pay in complexity and in fairness questions when they share a link with loss-based flows.
