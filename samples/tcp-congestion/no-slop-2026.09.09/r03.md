TCP congestion control is a feedback loop where the sender guesses how much data the network path can hold, sends that much, and adjusts the guess based on what comes back. The state variable is the congestion window (`cwnd`), a byte count of unacknowledged data the sender is willing to have in flight. The sender is limited by `min(cwnd, rwnd)`, where `rwnd` is the receiver's advertised buffer space. Flow control protects the receiver; congestion control protects the network. They are separate limits that happen to multiply into the same send decision.

## The signal

The network does not tell TCP its capacity. The sender infers it:

- **Packet loss.** Three duplicate ACKs, or a retransmission timeout. Historically loss meant a full queue, since bit errors were rare on wired links.
- **Delay.** Rising round-trip time means a queue is building ahead of you. Vegas, and later BBR, use this.
- **ECN.** Routers set a bit in the IP header instead of dropping. The receiver echoes it in the ACK. This gives a congestion signal without losing data, but requires router support.

## Slow start

A new connection has no idea what the path supports, so it probes exponentially. Start `cwnd` at 10 MSS (RFC 6928; it used to be 2 to 4). Every ACK that covers new data increases `cwnd` by one MSS, which doubles the window every round trip. This continues until `cwnd` crosses the slow start threshold (`ssthresh`) or loss occurs. "Slow" refers to the starting point, not the growth rate; the growth is the fastest thing TCP does.

## Congestion avoidance and AIMD

Past `ssthresh`, growth goes linear: `cwnd` increases by roughly one MSS per round trip, implemented as `cwnd += MSS*MSS/cwnd` per ACK. On loss, `cwnd` is halved. That pattern, additive increase and multiplicative decrease, is the core of classic TCP. Chiu and Jain showed in 1989 that AIMD converges to a fair, efficient allocation among flows sharing a bottleneck, and that additive decrease or multiplicative increase do not. The sawtooth you see in a `cwnd` plot is this rule running.

## Fast retransmit and fast recovery

Waiting for a retransmission timeout wastes an entire RTO, which is conservative by design. Instead, three duplicate ACKs are treated as a loss signal: retransmit immediately, set `ssthresh = cwnd/2`, set `cwnd = ssthresh`, and continue in congestion avoidance rather than dropping to 1 and re-entering slow start. That distinction is Reno versus Tahoe. NewReno fixed Reno's handling of multiple losses in one window. SACK lets the receiver report exactly which blocks arrived, so the sender retransmits only the holes.

A timeout is treated as much worse than duplicate ACKs. It collapses `cwnd` to 1 MSS, re-enters slow start, and doubles the RTO for each successive failure. The assumption is that the ACK clock is dead, so the sender has no information at all.

## What actually runs today

**CUBIC** is the Linux and Windows default. It replaces linear growth with a cubic function of time since the last loss, which flattens out near the previous window size (the probable capacity) and then grows aggressively past it. The important property is that growth depends on elapsed time, not on ACK arrivals, so a flow with a 200 ms RTT is not starved by a flow with a 10 ms RTT on the same bottleneck. Standard Reno is grossly unfair across RTTs and cannot fill a high bandwidth-delay-product path, since it needs an unrealistically low loss rate to keep a large window.

**BBR** takes a different approach: it estimates the bottleneck bandwidth and the minimum RTT directly, then sends at that rate with a small quantity of data in flight. It ignores loss as a primary signal. On paths with deep router buffers, loss-based algorithms fill those buffers before they get feedback, adding hundreds of milliseconds of queueing delay to everything sharing the link. BBR is designed to avoid that, though its fairness against CUBIC has been contentious and BBRv2 and v3 exist largely to address it.

## Two details that matter in practice

Sending is clocked by returning ACKs, which naturally spaces packets at the bottleneck rate. When that clock breaks (after an idle period, or with ACK compression), a sender can dump a full window as one burst and overflow a queue that had room. Modern stacks add pacing to spread transmissions over the RTT rather than relying on the ACK clock alone.

The algorithm also controls the queue, not just the send rate. A loss-based sender's steady state is a full bottleneck buffer, because that is what generates its feedback. Fixing the resulting latency requires the router side too, which is what CoDel and FQ-CoDel do.
