TCP congestion control is a feedback loop that guesses how much data the network path can hold, sends that much, and adjusts based on what comes back. The sender keeps a congestion window (`cwnd`) counting unacknowledged bytes it's allowed to have in flight. The actual limit is `min(cwnd, rwnd)`, where `rwnd` is the receiver's advertised flow-control window. Flow control protects the receiver; congestion control protects the network between them.

## The signal

The network gives no explicit "slow down" message by default, so classic TCP infers congestion from packet loss. Routers with full queues drop packets, the sender notices the missing ACK, and treats that as evidence it exceeded path capacity. This inference is why TCP performs badly on links with random (non-congestive) loss such as early wireless.

ACKs also serve as a clock: each arriving ACK frees window space and releases new data at roughly the rate the bottleneck drained the old data. This is why TCP self-paces even without a timer.

## The four phases (Reno, the baseline everyone else is described against)

**Slow start.** Begin with `cwnd` of about 10 segments (RFC 6928; older stacks used 1 to 4). Increase `cwnd` by one segment per ACK, which doubles it every round trip. Exponential growth, badly named. It ends when `cwnd` reaches `ssthresh` or loss occurs.

**Congestion avoidance.** Increase `cwnd` by roughly one segment per round trip instead of per ACK. Linear probing for more bandwidth.

**Fast retransmit.** Three duplicate ACKs mean a segment is missing but later ones arrived, so the path still works. Retransmit immediately rather than waiting for the retransmission timeout.

**Fast recovery.** Halve `ssthresh` to `cwnd/2`, set `cwnd` near that, and resume congestion avoidance without dropping back to slow start. A retransmission timeout, by contrast, is treated as severe congestion: `cwnd` collapses to one segment and slow start restarts.

Together these are additive increase, multiplicative decrease (AIMD): +1 segment per RTT while things are fine, ×0.5 on loss. AIMD provably converges to fair sharing among flows with equal round-trip times, and it's the reason a TCP throughput graph looks like a sawtooth.

## Why the classic algorithm doesn't scale

Reno's throughput is roughly `MSS / (RTT × √p)` for loss probability `p`. Filling a 10 Gbps link at 100 ms RTT requires a loss rate below about 10⁻¹⁰, and recovering from one loss takes over an hour of linear growth. Modern algorithms exist to fix this.

## What actually runs today

**CUBIC** (Linux default, and Windows since 10): `cwnd` grows as a cubic function of time since the last loss, independent of RTT. It rises fast toward the previous window size, flattens near it (the plateau where it suspects capacity is), then probes upward aggressively. RTT-independence makes it fairer across paths of differing latency than Reno.

**BBR** (Google, used for most YouTube and Google traffic) abandons loss as the signal. It continuously estimates bottleneck bandwidth and minimum RTT, then paces sending at bandwidth × minimum RTT. Because it targets the bandwidth-delay product rather than a full queue, it avoids bufferbloat: the pathology where oversized router buffers absorb everything, so loss-based TCP fills them and adds seconds of latency without losing a packet. BBR's fairness against CUBIC is contested, and v1 was known to starve competing loss-based flows in some configurations.

**Vegas** and its descendants use rising RTT as an early congestion signal. Pure delay-based flows lose to loss-based flows sharing a bottleneck, because the loss-based flow keeps pushing while the delay-based one backs off. This is why Vegas never displaced Reno.

**ECN** gets the router to mark packets instead of dropping them, giving an explicit congestion signal without loss. DCTCP extends this in data centers by reacting proportionally to the fraction of marked packets rather than binary-halving.

## Practical notes

- Congestion control is sender-side and unilateral. You can change it per-socket on Linux with `TCP_CONGESTION`, or globally via `net.ipv4.tcp_congestion_control`.
- SACK (selective acknowledgment) is essentially mandatory now; without it, recovering from multiple losses in one window takes a round trip each.
- QUIC reimplements all of this in userspace over UDP, with CUBIC and BBR as the usual choices, plus unambiguous packet numbering that removes the retransmission ambiguity TCP has to work around.
