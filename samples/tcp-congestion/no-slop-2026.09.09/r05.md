TCP congestion control is a feedback loop that guesses how much data the network path can hold, by increasing send rate until something breaks and then backing off.

## The core mechanism

The sender keeps a congestion window (`cwnd`), a byte count of unacknowledged data it's allowed to have in flight. The actual send limit is `min(cwnd, rwnd)`, where `rwnd` is the receiver's advertised buffer space. Flow control (`rwnd`) protects the receiver; congestion control (`cwnd`) protects the network. Nothing in IP tells the sender the path capacity, so `cwnd` is inferred entirely from ACK timing and loss.

## The classic four phases (Reno)

**Slow start.** `cwnd` starts around 10 segments and doubles every round trip: each ACK bumps it by one MSS. Exponential growth, despite the name. It ends when `cwnd` crosses the slow-start threshold (`ssthresh`) or a loss occurs.

**Congestion avoidance.** Above `ssthresh`, growth becomes linear: roughly +1 MSS per RTT. This is the additive increase half of AIMD (additive increase, multiplicative decrease).

**Fast retransmit.** Three duplicate ACKs for the same sequence number imply a single segment was dropped while later ones arrived. The sender retransmits immediately instead of waiting for the retransmission timeout.

**Fast recovery.** After that retransmit, the sender halves `cwnd` and `ssthresh` rather than collapsing to 1. That's the multiplicative decrease. A full RTO, by contrast, means the sender lost its ACK clock entirely: `cwnd` drops to 1 MSS and slow start restarts.

AIMD is what makes competing flows converge to a fair share. Additive increase moves flows toward equal rates; multiplicative decrease cuts everyone proportionally, so the ratio between a greedy flow and a small one shrinks each cycle.

## Why modern stacks aren't Reno

Reno's sawtooth throughput scales as roughly `MSS / (RTT * sqrt(p))` for loss rate `p`. On a 10 Gbps, 100 ms path, filling the pipe requires a packet loss rate near 10⁻¹⁰ and takes over an hour to recover from one drop. Two replacements dominate:

- **CUBIC** (Linux default since 2.6.19, also Windows since 2019): window growth follows a cubic function of time since the last loss, independent of RTT. It ramps fast, flattens near the previous loss point, then probes past it. RTT-independence also stops short-RTT flows from starving long ones.
- **BBR** (Google, used on YouTube and QUIC): ignores loss as the primary signal. It measures the maximum delivery rate and minimum RTT observed, then paces at the estimated bottleneck bandwidth. This avoids bufferbloat, where a deep router queue fills with packets that add latency without adding throughput, and loss-based algorithms keep pushing because no drops have happened yet.

## Explicit signals

ECN lets a router mark packets instead of dropping them; the receiver echoes the mark and the sender reduces `cwnd` as if it saw a loss, with no retransmission needed. This requires router support (usually paired with an active queue management scheme like CoDel or FQ-CoDel) and both endpoints negotiating ECN at handshake. DCTCP extends this in datacenters by reacting proportionally to the *fraction* of marked packets, giving much smaller queue oscillation than a flat halving.
