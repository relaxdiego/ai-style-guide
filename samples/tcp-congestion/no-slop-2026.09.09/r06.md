TCP congestion control regulates how fast a sender injects data into the network by maintaining a congestion window (cwnd): the number of unacknowledged bytes allowed in flight. The sender transmits at most `min(cwnd, receiver's advertised window)` and grows or shrinks cwnd based on feedback from the network. There is no explicit signal from routers in the classic design, so the sender infers congestion from packet loss and round-trip time.

## The core loop

The window is self-clocking. Each ACK returning from the receiver tells the sender that one packet left the network, which permits sending another. Throughput therefore settles at roughly `cwnd / RTT`. Congestion control is the algorithm that picks cwnd.

## Classic phases (Reno / NewReno)

**Slow start.** cwnd begins at about 10 packets (IW10, up from 1 or 2 in older stacks) and doubles every RTT: each ACK increases cwnd by one segment. This is exponential probing to find the available capacity quickly. It ends when cwnd crosses `ssthresh` (slow start threshold) or a loss occurs.

**Congestion avoidance.** Past ssthresh, cwnd grows by roughly one segment per RTT instead of doubling: each ACK adds `MSS/cwnd`. This is the additive increase of AIMD (additive increase, multiplicative decrease).

**Fast retransmit and fast recovery.** Three duplicate ACKs indicate a single lost packet while later packets still arrive. The sender retransmits immediately without waiting for a timeout, halves cwnd and ssthresh, and continues in congestion avoidance. NewReno and SACK extend this to recover multiple losses per window without collapsing.

**Retransmission timeout.** If the retransmission timer fires, the sender assumes severe congestion: cwnd drops to 1 segment, ssthresh is halved, and slow start restarts. The timer backs off exponentially.

AIMD is what makes the whole thing stable and fair. Chiu and Jain showed that additive increase with multiplicative decrease converges to an equal share among flows sharing a bottleneck, whereas other increase/decrease combinations do not. The characteristic sawtooth in a throughput graph is cwnd climbing linearly and being halved on each loss.

## What the classic design gets wrong

Loss as a congestion signal only fires after a router queue has already filled. On links with deep buffers, queues sit full and RTT balloons into hundreds of milliseconds while throughput is fine. That is bufferbloat. Loss-based control also scales badly on high bandwidth-delay-product paths: Reno needs an extremely low loss rate to keep a 10 Gbps window full, since it only adds one segment per RTT after each halving.

## Modern algorithms

- **CUBIC** (Linux default since 2006) replaces linear growth with a cubic function of time since the last loss. It approaches the previous window size cautiously, plateaus there, then probes aggressively past it. Growth is independent of RTT, which makes it fairer between short and long paths and much faster to fill big pipes. It also cuts by 0.7 rather than 0.5.
- **BBR** (Google) ignores loss entirely as a primary signal. It estimates the bottleneck bandwidth and minimum RTT separately, then paces sending at the bandwidth-delay product. This keeps queues near empty, so it avoids bufferbloat and tolerates random loss on wireless links. Its fairness against CUBIC has been contested; BBRv2 and v3 add loss and ECN response to address that.
- **ECN** lets routers mark packets instead of dropping them, giving the sender a congestion signal before any queue overflows. DCTCP uses the fraction of marked packets to scale the reduction proportionally rather than halving, which is why it works well in datacenters.
- **Vegas** and other delay-based schemes infer congestion from rising RTT. They achieve low latency but lose out to loss-based flows sharing a link, since a loss-based flow keeps pushing while Vegas backs off.

## Practical notes

Pacing matters as much as the window on modern stacks: sending a full window as a burst causes loss that a paced sender avoids. Linux exposes the algorithm via `net.ipv4.tcp_congestion_control` and pluggable modules, so you can switch to BBR per-socket or system-wide. QUIC reimplements all of this in userspace, typically with CUBIC or BBR, and gets better loss recovery because its packet numbers never repeat under retransmission.
