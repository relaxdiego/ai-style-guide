TCP congestion control is the sender's guess about how much data the network can hold, adjusted continuously from feedback. The network never tells the sender a rate; the sender infers one from ACK arrivals, duplicate ACKs, timeouts, and (sometimes) RTT changes.

## The core state

The sender keeps a congestion window (`cwnd`) in bytes. It may have at most `min(cwnd, receiver_window)` bytes unacknowledged in flight at any moment. Throughput is roughly `cwnd / RTT`, so controlling the window controls the rate. A second variable, `ssthresh`, marks where the sender switches from aggressive probing to cautious probing.

## Classic Reno/NewReno loop

**Slow start.** `cwnd` begins around 10 MSS (IW10, RFC 6928) and increases by 1 MSS per ACK, doubling per RTT. Exponential, despite the name; it is slow only relative to blasting at line rate. It ends when `cwnd` passes `ssthresh` or a loss occurs.

**Congestion avoidance.** `cwnd` grows by about 1 MSS per RTT (implemented as `cwnd += MSS²/cwnd` per ACK). Linear probing for spare capacity.

**Fast retransmit and fast recovery.** Three duplicate ACKs mean a segment is missing but later ones arrived, so the path still works. The sender retransmits immediately, sets `ssthresh = cwnd/2`, drops `cwnd` to that, and continues. Linux uses Proportional Rate Reduction (RFC 6937) to glide down to the new window instead of chopping it in one step, which avoids a stall.

**Timeout.** No ACK before the retransmission timer fires means severe trouble. `cwnd` collapses to 1 MSS and slow start restarts. This is the expensive case, and RTO is deliberately conservative (minimum 200ms in Linux, 1s in the RFC).

That pattern is additive increase, multiplicative decrease. Chiu and Jain showed AIMD converges to a fair, efficient allocation among flows sharing a bottleneck, which is why the decrease is a factor and the increase is a constant rather than the reverse.

## Why the classics were replaced

Reno's linear growth is too slow on high bandwidth-delay-product paths. Recovering to a 10 Gbps window across a 100ms RTT after one loss takes on the order of an hour. Reno also treats every loss as congestion, which punishes wireless links that drop packets for radio reasons.

**CUBIC** (Linux default since 2.6.19) replaces the linear increase with a cubic function of time since the last reduction. It rises fast, flattens near the window size where loss last happened, then probes aggressively past it. Growth depends on elapsed time rather than ACK count, so short-RTT and long-RTT flows compete more evenly. Its decrease factor is 0.7, not 0.5.

**BBR** (Google, deployed widely) abandons loss as the signal. It estimates the bottleneck bandwidth from delivery rate and the minimum RTT from observed samples, then paces at that product. It periodically probes higher and drains back. The motivation is bufferbloat: with large router buffers, loss-based control fills the buffer before it backs off, adding hundreds of milliseconds of queueing delay for no throughput gain. BBR aims to sit at the knee instead of the cliff. Fairness between BBR and CUBIC on a shared bottleneck remains contested, and BBRv3 exists partly to address that.

**ECN and DCTCP** get an explicit signal instead of inferring one. Routers mark packets when queues build, the receiver echoes the mark, and the sender reduces before anything is lost. DCTCP goes further and scales the reduction to the fraction of marked packets, giving very shallow queues. Both need cooperating network equipment, so ECN is common in datacenters and patchy on the public internet.

## Two things people commonly get wrong

Congestion control and flow control are separate. Flow control is the receiver's advertised window protecting its own buffer. Congestion control protects the network. The sender obeys the smaller of the two.

Modern senders also pace: rather than dumping a whole window as a burst when ACKs arrive, they spread transmissions over the RTT. Bursts create transient queues and losses that the window size alone would not predict. BBR requires pacing; CUBIC in Linux uses it too via `fq` or internal pacing.
