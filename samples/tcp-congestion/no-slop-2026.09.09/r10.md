TCP congestion control limits how much unacknowledged data a sender puts on the network, so that senders collectively don't push more traffic into a link than it can carry. Flow control (the receiver's advertised window) is a separate mechanism protecting the receiver's buffer; congestion control protects the network. The sender may have at most `min(cwnd, rwnd)` bytes in flight, where `cwnd` is the congestion window the sender computes itself.

## The core loop

The sender has no direct view of queue depth inside routers, so it infers congestion from signals that come back: an ACK means a packet left the network, a loss or a delay increase means a queue somewhere is full or filling. It then adjusts `cwnd` and repeats. This is a control loop with a delay of one round-trip time, which is why the RTT estimate matters so much.

## Classic algorithms (Reno, RFC 5681)

**Slow start.** Begin with `cwnd` around 10 MSS. Increase `cwnd` by one MSS per ACK received, which doubles the window every RTT. Exponential growth, despite the name; "slow" is relative to the old behavior of blasting a full receiver window immediately. Continue until `cwnd` reaches `ssthresh` or a loss occurs.

**Congestion avoidance.** Above `ssthresh`, increase `cwnd` by roughly one MSS per RTT instead of per ACK (in practice, `cwnd += MSS*MSS/cwnd` per ACK). Linear growth, probing for more capacity cautiously.

**Fast retransmit.** Three duplicate ACKs mean a segment is missing but later segments arrived, so the path is still delivering. Retransmit the missing segment immediately rather than waiting for the retransmission timeout.

**Fast recovery.** After fast retransmit, halve `ssthresh` to `cwnd/2`, set `cwnd` to the new `ssthresh`, and keep transmitting on incoming duplicate ACKs. Contrast with a retransmission timeout, which is treated as severe: `cwnd` drops to 1 MSS and slow start restarts.

Together these produce **AIMD**: additive increase, multiplicative decrease. Chiu and Jain showed AIMD converges to a fair, efficient allocation among flows sharing a bottleneck, which is the reason for the asymmetry between the two. The steady-state result is the well-known throughput relation, roughly `MSS / (RTT * sqrt(p))` for loss rate `p`, meaning throughput falls with RTT and with the square root of loss.

## What runs in practice

**CUBIC** is the Linux and Windows default. It replaces the linear increase with a cubic function of time since the last loss event. The window grows fast when far below the pre-loss value, flattens as it approaches it, then grows fast again while probing higher. Because growth depends on elapsed time rather than ACK arrivals, CUBIC is far less penalized on high-bandwidth, high-latency paths where Reno's one-MSS-per-RTT crawl is hopeless.

**BBR** takes a different approach: estimate the bottleneck bandwidth and the minimum RTT, and pace sending at that bandwidth-delay product instead of reacting to loss. This is aimed at bufferbloat, where deep router buffers absorb the excess a loss-based sender creates, so loss arrives only after latency has already ballooned. BBR keeps queues short but its fairness against CUBIC has been contentious; BBRv2 and v3 add loss and ECN response to address that.

**ECN** lets a router mark a packet instead of dropping it when its queue builds. The receiver echoes the mark, the sender reduces its window as if there had been a loss, and no retransmission is needed. DCTCP and L4S extend this to fine-grained marking, reacting proportionally to how often marks arrive rather than treating each one as a full congestion event.

## Things that trip people up

- Loss is a proxy for congestion, and a bad one on wireless links where packets are lost to interference. This is why loss-based control underperforms on lossy paths.
- Halving the window on a single loss costs an RTT to recover, which on a satellite link is hundreds of milliseconds.
- **SACK** matters a lot: without it, a sender losing multiple segments in one window learns about one loss per RTT.
- Congestion control operates per connection, so opening many parallel connections gets you a larger share of a bottleneck. HTTP/2 and HTTP/3 multiplexing partly gives that back through a single connection.
- QUIC moves congestion control into userspace over UDP, which is why new algorithms deploy there faster than in kernel TCP stacks.
