TCP congestion control is a feedback loop: the sender has no idea how much capacity the path has, so it increases its sending rate until the network signals distress, then cuts back. Everything else is detail about how it ramps up and what counts as distress.

## The state the sender keeps

The sender maintains a congestion window (`cwnd`), the number of bytes it may have in flight unacknowledged. Actual send limit is `min(cwnd, rwnd)`, where `rwnd` is the receiver's advertised window (flow control, a separate concern about receiver buffer space). A second variable, `ssthresh`, marks the boundary between the aggressive and cautious ramp-up modes.

Because a window of W bytes drains over one round trip, throughput is roughly `cwnd / RTT`. That relationship is why RTT shows up everywhere below.

## Slow start

Connections open with `cwnd` at about 10 MSS on modern stacks (RFC 6928; the original was 1 to 2). Every ACK bumps `cwnd` by one MSS, so the window doubles each round trip. Exponential growth, despite the name: "slow" is relative to the original TCP, which just blasted out a full receiver window.

Slow start ends when `cwnd` reaches `ssthresh` or a loss occurs.

## Congestion avoidance

Now growth goes linear: `cwnd` increases by roughly one MSS per RTT, implemented per-ACK as `cwnd += MSS * MSS / cwnd`. The sender is creeping upward looking for the point where the bottleneck queue overflows.

## Reacting to loss

Two loss signals, treated very differently.

**Three duplicate ACKs** mean a packet vanished but later packets are still arriving, so the path is basically alive. The sender does fast retransmit (resend the missing segment immediately rather than waiting for a timer) and fast recovery. Reno sets `ssthresh = cwnd / 2` and resumes congestion avoidance from there.

**Retransmission timeout** means no useful ACKs came back at all. This is treated as severe: `ssthresh = cwnd / 2`, `cwnd` drops to 1 MSS, and slow start begins again. The RTO itself is computed from a smoothed RTT estimate plus a variance term (Jacobson/Karels), and doubles on each successive timeout.

The additive-increase/multiplicative-decrease shape is not arbitrary. Chiu and Jain showed that linear increase with proportional decrease converges to an equal split among flows sharing a bottleneck, which is why AIMD survived while other rules did not.

## What actually runs today

**CUBIC** is the Linux and Windows default. After a reduction it grows `cwnd` as a cubic function of elapsed time since that reduction, flattening near the previous window value and then probing aggressively past it. Growth depends on wall-clock time rather than ACK arrivals, which removes Reno's bias against long-RTT flows and lets it fill high bandwidth-delay-product paths that Reno would take minutes to ramp up on. Its backoff factor is 0.7, gentler than Reno's 0.5.

**BBR** takes a different approach entirely. It estimates the bottleneck bandwidth and the minimum RTT, then paces packets at that estimated bandwidth with a small window cap. Loss is not the primary signal. This matters because loss-based algorithms by construction fill the bottleneck queue before backing off, and deep buffers in home routers and mobile networks turn that into hundreds of milliseconds of standing latency (bufferbloat). BBR aims for high throughput without the queue. Fairness between BBR and CUBIC flows on the same link is still contested; BBRv1 was notably rough on CUBIC in shallow-buffer cases, and v2/v3 exist largely to fix that.

**ECN** lets routers mark packets instead of dropping them, giving the sender the same signal without the loss. DCTCP extends this inside datacenters by reacting proportionally to the fraction of marked packets rather than treating any mark as a full congestion event.

**Loss detection** has also moved on: SACK tells the sender exactly which segments arrived, and RACK-TLP replaces the duplicate-ACK counting heuristic with a time-based rule that handles reordering far better.

## The parts people trip over

Congestion control and flow control are separate mechanisms that both limit the send rate; `rwnd` protects the receiver, `cwnd` protects the network.

Modern senders also pace packets rather than releasing a whole window as a burst, since a burst that fits the window can still overflow a shallow buffer.

And the loop is per-connection, so an application opening six parallel connections gets roughly six times the share of a competing single-connection flow. That is a large part of why HTTP/2 multiplexing over one connection changed performance characteristics, and why QUIC moved congestion control into userspace where it can be iterated on without kernel deployment cycles.
