TCP congestion control is a feedback loop in which the sender continually guesses how much unacknowledged data the path can hold, and revises that guess downward whenever it sees evidence the guess was too high. The network itself tells the sender almost nothing directly: routers do not advertise their capacity or the state of their queues, so the sender has to infer both from the only signals that come back to it, which are acknowledgements, their timing, and their absence. Everything that follows is a consequence of having to run a control loop on such thin evidence.

The state variable is the congestion window, `cwnd`, held at the sender and never transmitted on the wire. The sender may have at most `min(cwnd, rwnd)` bytes in flight, where `rwnd` is the receiver's advertised window; the congestion window protects the network while the advertised window protects the receiver's buffer. Because a window of data is emitted once per round trip, `cwnd/RTT` is the effective sending rate, and adjusting the window is how the sender adjusts its rate without ever thinking in terms of bytes per second.

A new connection has no idea what the path can carry, so it begins in slow start, which despite the name is the aggressive phase. The window starts at around ten segments (RFC 6928 raised this from the older values of two to four) and grows by one segment for every segment acknowledged, which doubles it every round trip. Exponential growth means a connection finds a gigabit path in a couple of dozen round trips rather than thousands, and it is the reason short web transfers, which often finish inside slow start, never reach the steady-state behaviour that the rest of the algorithm describes.

Slow start ends either at a threshold, `ssthresh`, or when something goes wrong, and the connection moves into congestion avoidance, where the window grows by roughly one segment per round trip instead of doubling. Loss, when it appears, cuts the window by a multiplicative factor. This pairing of additive increase with multiplicative decrease is not arbitrary: Chiu and Jain showed in 1989 that among linear control rules, only this combination drives competing flows toward an equal share of a bottleneck, because the additive term is the same for everyone in absolute size while the multiplicative term takes more from whoever has more. The cost is the sawtooth, since a flow in steady state is always either probing above the correct rate or recovering below it, and never simply sitting at it.

How the sender learns about loss matters as much as what it does about it. Three duplicate acknowledgements indicate that segments after a gap are still arriving, so the sender retransmits the missing segment immediately rather than waiting for a timer, halves `cwnd`, and continues in fast recovery, which is Reno's contribution over the original Tahoe. A retransmission timeout means nothing is getting through at all; the sender collapses `cwnd` to one segment and re-enters slow start, which is why a timeout is far more expensive than a fast retransmit and why so much engineering has gone into avoiding one. NewReno handles multiple losses in a single window without repeatedly cutting, and selective acknowledgement (SACK) lets the receiver report exactly which blocks arrived, so the sender retransmits only what is actually missing.

Halving on every loss becomes untenable on long fat paths. A flow doing 10 Gbit/s across an intercontinental link with a 100 ms round trip needs a window of tens of thousands of segments, and recovering that window at one segment per round trip after a halving takes over an hour, which means a single random loss costs an enormous amount of throughput. CUBIC, the Linux default since 2006 and now the most widely deployed algorithm on the internet, addresses this by making the window a cubic function of the time elapsed since the last reduction rather than a function of acknowledgements received. The curve rises fast after the cut, flattens as it approaches the window size where loss last occurred, and then accelerates again to probe for new capacity. Because the growth is driven by wall-clock time, flows with short and long round trips grow at comparable rates, which removes Reno's bias in favour of nearby senders; CUBIC also cuts to 0.7 of the window rather than 0.5.

All of these treat loss as the congestion signal, and that has an unfortunate consequence. Loss only happens once a queue has filled, so a loss-based sender is not finding the path's capacity but rather filling the bottleneck buffer until it overflows, and standing queues of hundreds of milliseconds are the result. This is bufferbloat, and it is why a large download can make an interactive session on the same link unusable. BBR, developed at Google and now in its third revision, abandons loss as the primary signal and instead builds a model of the path: it separately estimates the bottleneck bandwidth from the maximum delivery rate observed and the propagation delay from the minimum round trip observed, then paces its output at the estimated bandwidth with only a small amount of data in flight beyond the bandwidth-delay product. It probes periodically by sending faster for a round trip and watching whether delivery rate rises or only queueing delay does. The design keeps buffers largely empty, but it makes BBR's behaviour when sharing a bottleneck with CUBIC a matter of ongoing argument and revision.

Two other pieces belong in the picture. Explicit Congestion Notification lets a router mark a packet instead of dropping it, giving the sender the same information without the retransmission, and the L4S work extends this into a much finer-grained signal that lets algorithms like DCTCP hold queues at a few packets; the historical obstacle has been middleboxes that mangle the bits rather than any defect in the idea. On the router side, active queue management schemes such as CoDel and FQ_CoDel attack the problem from the other end by dropping or marking early based on how long packets have been queued, which limits the damage any single greedy sender can do. Congestion control is finally a distributed resource allocation performed by mutually suspicious endpoints with no coordinator, and its stability for forty years is a genuinely surprising fact about a system nobody designed centrally.
