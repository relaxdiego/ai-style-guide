TCP congestion control is the mechanism by which a sender discovers how fast it can transmit without overwhelming the network, using almost no information from the network itself. The endpoints cannot see the routers between them, so they cannot know the bottleneck link's capacity or how many other flows are competing for it. The whole design follows from that blindness: the sender probes upward until it sees evidence that it has pushed too hard, backs off, and probes again. What counts as evidence, and how hard to back off, is essentially the entire design space.

The state variable is the congestion window, `cwnd`, a sender-side limit on how many bytes may be in flight (sent but not yet acknowledged). It sits alongside the receiver's advertised window, and the sender is bound by the smaller of the two. Those two limits solve different problems and it's worth keeping them apart: the receive window is flow control, protecting a slow receiver from a fast sender, while `cwnd` is congestion control, protecting the network from everybody. The window also gives the algorithm its clock. Because a new segment goes out roughly when an old one is acknowledged, the ACK stream paces transmission at the rate the bottleneck is actually draining, which is the self-clocking property Van Jacobson built on after the 1986 congestion collapse on the NSFNET backbone, when throughput on one link fell by three orders of magnitude.

A connection begins in slow start, where `cwnd` starts at around ten segments in modern stacks and doubles every round trip, since each acknowledged segment releases two new ones. The name is historical and misleading: growth is exponential, and it is "slow" only compared to the earlier behaviour of immediately blasting a full window. Slow start's job is to find the rough scale of the path's capacity quickly, and it ends either when `cwnd` crosses a threshold, `ssthresh`, remembered from earlier congestion, or when loss appears. After that the connection enters congestion avoidance, where it adds roughly one segment per round trip rather than doubling, and on detecting loss it cuts the window multiplicatively. This additive-increase, multiplicative-decrease rule is not arbitrary. Chiu and Jain showed that among linear control rules, AIMD is the one that drives competing flows toward an equal share: additive increase moves flows along a line parallel to the fairness diagonal, multiplicative decrease moves them back along a ray through the origin, and iterating the two converges on equal allocation regardless of where the flows started.

How the sender learns about congestion matters as much as how it reacts. A retransmission timeout is the pessimistic signal, taken to mean the pipe has emptied and the ACK clock is lost, so `cwnd` collapses to one segment and slow start begins again. Three duplicate ACKs are the optimistic signal: packets are still arriving at the receiver, so one segment was lost but the clock survives. TCP Reno retransmits immediately without waiting for the timer, halves the window, and resumes congestion avoidance, which is fast retransmit followed by fast recovery. Selective acknowledgement extends this by letting the receiver report exactly which blocks arrived, so a sender losing several segments in one window can repair them together instead of one per round trip.

Treating a single drop as the congestion signal has a consequence that shaped everything after Reno. Steady-state throughput works out to roughly `MSS / (RTT · √p)` for loss rate `p`, so sustaining ten gigabits per second across a 100 ms path requires a loss rate below one packet in a billion, and after any single loss a Reno flow needs tens of minutes to climb back. Modern algorithms attack this from different directions:

| | Congestion signal | Increase | Decrease |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reno | Loss (timeout or 3 dup-ACKs) | +1 MSS per RTT | ×0.5 |
| CUBIC | Loss | Cubic in time since last loss, independent of RTT | ×0.7 |
| BBR | Measured bandwidth and min RTT | Paces at estimated BDP, periodic probing | No loss-triggered cut |

CUBIC, the Linux and Windows default, makes window growth a cubic function of elapsed time since the last reduction rather than a count of round trips. Far below the previous maximum it grows aggressively, flattens as it approaches that maximum, and accelerates again if it passes through unharmed. Decoupling growth from RTT also means a flow crossing a continent competes on more equal terms with a flow crossing a data centre, which Reno's per-RTT increment badly penalises.

Explicit Congestion Notification changes the signal rather than the response: routers mark a bit in the IP header when their queues build, and the receiver echoes the mark back, so the sender learns about congestion without a packet having to die for it. That matters because loss-based control has a structural side effect known as bufferbloat. A Reno or CUBIC flow only backs off once a queue has overflowed, so it works by keeping the bottleneck buffer full, and on paths with deep buffers that means hundreds of milliseconds of standing queue delay for every flow sharing the link. The two responses are active queue management in the routers, where CoDel and FQ-CoDel drop or mark based on how long packets have been sitting rather than on buffer occupancy, and rate-based control at the sender.

BBR is the main example of the latter. Instead of inferring congestion from loss, it continuously estimates the bottleneck bandwidth from delivery rate and the propagation delay from the minimum RTT observed, then paces transmission at their product, periodically probing higher to check whether more bandwidth has appeared and occasionally draining to re-measure the true minimum RTT. Because it targets the bandwidth-delay product rather than buffer overflow, it can hold high throughput on lossy paths and leave queues comparatively short. Its fairness against CUBIC has been genuinely contested: the first version could crowd out loss-based flows on deep-buffered links and behaved poorly when several BBR flows shared a shallow one, and BBRv2 and v3 fold loss and ECN back in as secondary signals to address exactly that.
