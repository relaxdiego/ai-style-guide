TCP congestion control exists because a sender has no way to ask the network how much capacity is available. Nothing along the path tells it the bottleneck link speed or how many other flows are competing for it, so the sender has to discover that empirically: send at some rate, watch what comes back, and adjust. Everything else follows from that constraint.

The state variable doing the work is the congestion window, `cwnd`, which caps how many unacknowledged bytes may be in flight at once. The sender's actual limit is the minimum of `cwnd` and the receiver's advertised window, which separates the two distinct concerns of not overrunning the receiver's buffer and not overrunning the network. Because a window bounds bytes in flight rather than a rate, it produces a rate implicitly: roughly `cwnd / RTT`. Acknowledgements returning from the receiver also serve as a clock, since each ack frees window space and lets new data out, which self-regulates the sender to the pace at which data is actually draining through the bottleneck.

Classic TCP (Reno and its descendants) runs a small state machine over that window:

- **Slow start.** Begin with an initial window of about 10 segments and increase `cwnd` by one segment per ack, which doubles it every round trip. The name is misleading: the growth is exponential, and it is "slow" only relative to the older behaviour of immediately sending a full receiver window. This phase is a fast search for the right order of magnitude.
- **Congestion avoidance.** Once `cwnd` passes a threshold (`ssthresh`), growth switches to roughly one segment per round trip. This is the additive increase, a cautious probe upward on the assumption the sender is now near capacity.
- **Fast retransmit and fast recovery.** Three duplicate acks signal that a segment was lost but later ones arrived, so the sender retransmits immediately rather than waiting for a timeout, halves `cwnd`, and continues from there.
- **Timeout.** If the retransmission timer fires, the sender assumes something much worse, collapses `cwnd` to one segment, sets `ssthresh` to half the previous window, and re-enters slow start.

The increase and decrease rules together form additive increase, multiplicative decrease, and the asymmetry is deliberate. Chiu and Jain showed that flows sharing a bottleneck under AIMD converge toward an equal share, whereas symmetric rules do not: gentle probing upward and sharp backoff on congestion pushes competing flows toward fairness rather than letting an aggressive one keep its advantage. The signal being interpreted is packet loss, which works because the dominant cause of loss in wired networks is buffer overflow at a congested router rather than corruption.

That inference is also the weak point, and most modern work is a response to it. Reno's linear growth is far too slow to fill a high bandwidth-delay-product path, since recovering a large window one segment per RTT can take minutes on a fast intercontinental link. CUBIC, the Linux default, replaces the linear increase with a cubic function of the time elapsed since the last reduction: it climbs quickly back toward the window where loss previously occurred, flattens out around that point to probe cautiously, then accelerates again if no loss appears. Because the curve is parameterised by wall-clock time rather than by acks received, CUBIC also treats flows with short and long round trips more evenly, where Reno systematically favours short-RTT flows that get their additive increments more often.

A deeper objection is that loss-based control only backs off once a buffer is already full, so it drives every bottleneck queue to overflow by design and leaves a standing queue behind it. That is the bufferbloat problem: throughput stays fine while latency inflates, sometimes by hundreds of milliseconds, which is ruinous for interactive traffic sharing the link. BBR attacks this by not using loss as the primary signal at all. It maintains running estimates of the bottleneck bandwidth and the minimum round-trip time, periodically probing each, and paces transmission at the estimated bandwidth so the pipe stays full while the queue stays near empty. The tradeoff is that BBR's behaviour when competing with loss-based flows depends heavily on buffer sizing and on the version deployed, and getting that interaction right has driven most of the revisions since.

Explicit Congestion Notification offers a third path, where a router experiencing congestion marks a packet instead of dropping it and the receiver echoes the mark back, giving the sender the same information without the loss and retransmission. Classic ECN is treated as equivalent to a drop, so it saves the retransmission but not the queue. L4S extends the idea by marking far more aggressively and pairing it with congestion controllers that respond proportionally rather than by halving, aiming for consistently shallow queues on links that support it.
