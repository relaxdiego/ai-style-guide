TCP congestion control exists because a sender has no way to know how much capacity the path actually has. Flow control, the receiver's advertised window, only stops a fast sender from overrunning a slow receiver's buffers; it says nothing about the dozen routers in between. The shared resource in the middle is queue space at the bottleneck link, and if the senders collectively push more than that link can drain, queues build, latency climbs, and eventually packets are dropped. The congestion collapse episodes on the early Internet in 1986 were exactly this: throughput fell by orders of magnitude because retransmissions piled onto an already saturated network, and Van Jacobson's response is the ancestor of everything running today.

The mechanism is a second window kept privately by the sender, the congestion window or cwnd, with the amount of unacknowledged data in flight capped at the smaller of cwnd and the receiver's window. Because the classic network gives no explicit feedback, the sender has to infer capacity by probing: raise cwnd until something signals that the path is full, back off, and repeat forever. Acknowledgements do double duty here, since an arriving ACK both carries the congestion signal and tells the sender that a packet has left the network, which is what lets it clock a new one in.

Growth happens in two regimes. Slow start, which is only slow relative to blasting the receiver's whole window immediately, increases cwnd by one segment per ACK received, roughly doubling it every round trip, so a connection ramps from one segment to the path's capacity in logarithmic time. Once cwnd crosses a threshold called ssthresh, the sender switches to congestion avoidance and grows by about one segment per round trip instead, a cautious linear probe on the assumption that it is now near the operating point. On a loss, ssthresh is set to roughly half the current window so the next connection through the same territory knows where to stop being aggressive.

How the sender reacts to loss depends on how it learned about it. Three duplicate ACKs mean later packets are still arriving, so the path is working and one segment went missing: Reno retransmits immediately without waiting for the timer, halves cwnd, and continues from there, which is fast retransmit and fast recovery. A retransmission timeout means nothing is coming back at all, and the sender treats this as severe congestion, collapsing cwnd to one segment and re-entering slow start. Modern stacks soften the first case considerably with SACK, which tells the sender precisely which blocks arrived, and with RACK, which uses time rather than duplicate-ACK counting to decide a packet is lost.

The additive-increase, multiplicative-decrease pattern is not arbitrary. Chiu and Jain showed that among the simple linear control rules, AIMD is the one that drives competing flows toward an equal share while staying efficient: the additive part closes the gap between a large flow and a small one slowly, and the multiplicative part cuts the large flow harder in absolute terms every time congestion is detected. The steady-state consequence is the well-known throughput relation, in which a flow's rate is proportional to the segment size divided by the round-trip time and the square root of the loss rate. Two awkward properties fall straight out of that formula. Flows with short round trips beat flows with long ones over the same bottleneck, and a long-distance high-speed path needs an extraordinarily low loss rate to be filled at all, since a 10 Gb/s transfer across 100 ms of latency cannot be sustained by a rule that halves its window on every drop.

Those limits drove the algorithms in use now, which differ mainly in what they treat as the congestion signal:

- **CUBIC**, the Linux and Windows default, keeps loss as the signal but grows cwnd as a cubic function of the time since the last reduction, flattening out near the previous window and then probing aggressively past it. Because growth is driven by elapsed time rather than ACK arrivals, it is much fairer between flows with different round-trip times, and it scales to fast long paths.
- **BBR**, developed at Google, ignores loss and instead estimates the bottleneck bandwidth and the minimum round-trip time, then paces its sending at that bandwidth-delay product. It aims to sit at the point where the pipe is full but the queue is not, which keeps latency low, though its interaction with loss-based flows sharing a buffer has been a persistent point of contention across its versions.
- **Delay-based schemes** such as Vegas read rising round-trip time as the early warning that a queue is forming. They are gentle and low-latency in isolation, and they lose badly to loss-based flows, which are happy to fill the buffer the delay-based flow is trying to avoid.
- **ECN and DCTCP** replace inference with an explicit mark set by the router before it has to drop anything. DCTCP goes further by reacting in proportion to the fraction of marked packets rather than halving unconditionally, which works beautifully inside a datacenter where you control both endpoints and the switches.

One practical consequence of loss-based control is worth knowing about, since it explains a class of user-visible problem. A Reno or CUBIC flow only stops growing when a buffer overflows, so if the bottleneck router has a very large buffer, the flow will fill it and every other packet sharing that queue, including interactive traffic, inherits hundreds of milliseconds of standing delay. This is bufferbloat, and the fix lives in the network rather than the sender: active queue management such as CoDel and FQ-CoDel drops or marks early to keep the standing queue short, and fair queueing isolates the latency-sensitive flow from the bulk one. Senders help by pacing their transmissions across the round trip instead of releasing a window's worth in a burst, which is now standard in Linux and is essential to BBR.
