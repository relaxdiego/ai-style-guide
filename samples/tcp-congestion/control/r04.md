TCP congestion control is the mechanism by which a sender infers, without any explicit report from the network, how much data it can have in flight before it starts overloading the slowest link on the path. The network gives almost no feedback: routers do not tell you their queue depth, they just drop packets or delay them when a buffer fills. So the sender maintains its own guess, the congestion window (cwnd), and continually probes upward until something tells it the guess was too high, then backs off. The amount of unacknowledged data is bounded by the minimum of cwnd and the receiver's advertised window, so throughput is roughly cwnd divided by the round-trip time. Everything interesting is in how cwnd moves.

The classical algorithm, from Van Jacobson's response to the 1986 congestion collapse of the early Internet, has four coupled parts. Slow start begins with a small window (RFC 6928 raised the initial value to 10 segments) and doubles cwnd every round trip, which sounds aggressive but is the fastest safe way to find the right order of magnitude from below. When cwnd crosses a threshold called ssthresh, or when loss occurs, the sender switches to congestion avoidance and increases by about one segment per round trip instead, creeping up on capacity. Loss is then interpreted two ways depending on how it is detected. Three duplicate ACKs mean a packet was lost but later packets are still arriving, so the path is alive; fast retransmit resends the missing segment immediately and fast recovery halves cwnd rather than starting over. A retransmission timeout means nothing is getting through at all, and the sender collapses cwnd to one segment and re-enters slow start.

The additive-increase, multiplicative-decrease pattern in congestion avoidance is not arbitrary. Chiu and Jain showed that among the simple linear control laws, AIMD is the one that converges to a fair and efficient allocation when several flows share a bottleneck: the multiplicative decrease shrinks large windows more than small ones in absolute terms, which pulls competing flows together, while additive increase keeps them from all charging back to the ceiling in unison. The characteristic sawtooth you see in a cwnd trace is the algorithm working, not failing. It also means a loss-based flow deliberately overfills the bottleneck buffer on every cycle, since a drop is the only signal it gets, and that is the root of bufferbloat: with a large router buffer, the standing queue adds hundreds of milliseconds of latency to everything sharing the link.

Reno's behaviour has a well-known closed form: throughput scales as roughly MSS divided by RTT times the square root of the loss rate. That relationship falls apart on modern paths. Filling a 10 Gbps link at 100 ms RTT with 1500-byte packets needs a window of about 83,000 packets sustained, which requires a loss rate below about 10⁻¹⁰, far beyond what real hardware delivers. Reno also penalises long-RTT flows twice over, since it increases once per round trip. Both problems drove the algorithms in use today.

| | Congestion signal | Window response | Where it runs |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reno / NewReno | Packet loss | Halve on loss, +1 MSS per RTT | Reference behaviour, largely superseded |
| CUBIC | Packet loss | Cut to 0.7×, then grow as a cubic in elapsed time | Linux and Windows default |
| BBR | Measured bandwidth and min RTT | Pace at estimated bottleneck rate | Google services, QUIC, widely deployed |

CUBIC keeps the loss signal but changes the growth curve to a cubic function of the time since the last reduction, which is deliberately flat near the previous window size and steep far from it. Growth in wall-clock time rather than per-ACK makes it roughly RTT-fair, and the flat plateau lets it linger near the last known capacity before probing hard again. BBR abandons loss entirely as a signal, on the grounds that loss and congestion stopped being synonymous once buffers grew large and wireless links became lossy for unrelated reasons. It estimates the bottleneck bandwidth as the maximum delivery rate observed over a recent window, estimates the propagation delay as the minimum RTT over a longer one, and paces packets out at that rate so the queue never builds in the first place. This gives much better latency under load, at the cost of fairness arguments with CUBIC flows that are still busy filling buffers; BBRv2 and v3 exist largely to soften those interactions.

There is also an explicit path. ECN lets a router mark a packet rather than drop it, and DCTCP-style algorithms react in proportion to the fraction of marked packets rather than treating one mark as a full congestion event, which produces much smaller, smoother window adjustments. That works well in datacentres where you control both endpoints and the switches, and the L4S work is an attempt to make the same approach deployable across the public Internet with a separate low-latency queue.

Worth keeping in mind throughout: none of this is enforced. A sender that ignores congestion control entirely will simply get more bandwidth until routers drop enough of its traffic to matter. The system holds together because the dominant implementations, in a handful of operating systems and QUIC libraries, choose to cooperate.
