TCP congestion control is a sender-side control loop: the sender keeps a congestion window (`cwnd`), the number of bytes it may have in flight unacknowledged, and adjusts it based on feedback from ACKs. The receiver's advertised window (`rwnd`) limits what the receiver can buffer; the actual send limit is `min(cwnd, rwnd)`. Nothing in the network tells the sender its fair rate, so the sender probes upward until it sees a congestion signal, then backs off.

The classic loop (Reno, and the base for almost everything since) has four states:

- **Slow start.** `cwnd` begins at about 10 MSS (RFC 6928) and doubles every RTT, since each ACK bumps it by 1 MSS. Exponential growth, used on connection start and after a timeout. It ends when `cwnd` crosses `ssthresh` or a loss occurs.
- **Congestion avoidance.** `cwnd` grows by roughly 1 MSS per RTT (`cwnd += MSS²/cwnd` per ACK). Linear probing for spare capacity.
- **Fast retransmit / fast recovery.** Three duplicate ACKs mean a segment was lost but later ones arrived, so the path is still passing traffic. The sender retransmits immediately, sets `ssthresh = cwnd/2`, drops `cwnd` to about that, and continues in congestion avoidance. No return to slow start.
- **Retransmission timeout.** No ACK before the RTO fires. This is the pessimistic case: `ssthresh = cwnd/2`, `cwnd` back to the initial window, slow start again, and the RTO doubles on repeat.

Two things make this work beyond the local behaviour of one connection. Additive increase with multiplicative decrease converges to a fair split among flows sharing a bottleneck (the Chiu-Jain argument): increases add the same absolute amount to every flow while decreases cut proportionally, so the ratio between flows moves toward 1. And ACKs act as a clock: a sender transmits new data only as ACKs return, so its send rate self-adjusts to the bottleneck's drain rate without any explicit rate calculation. The steady-state throughput of loss-based TCP falls out as roughly `(MSS/RTT) · 1.22/√p` for loss probability `p`, which is why short-RTT flows beat long-RTT flows on the same link, and why a single flow cannot fill a fast, long path without very low loss.

Modern stacks have moved past Reno's response to loss:

| Algorithm | Signal | Behaviour | Where used |
|---|---|---|---|
| NewReno | Loss (dup ACKs) | Handles multiple losses per window via partial ACKs | Fallback baseline |
| CUBIC | Loss | Window is a cubic function of time since the last reduction, `β=0.7`; growth independent of RTT | Linux default, Android, most servers |
| BBR (v1-v3) | Bandwidth and RTT estimates | Models bottleneck bandwidth and minimum RTT, paces at that rate, probes periodically; v2/v3 also react to loss and ECN | Google, YouTube, increasingly common |
| Vegas | Delay (RTT increase) | Backs off before loss; loses badly when competing with loss-based flows | Research, rarely deployed alone |
| DCTCP | ECN mark fraction | Reduces proportionally to the fraction of marked packets, giving small queues | Datacenters with ECN-capable switches |

Two practical wrinkles matter in deployment. Loss-based algorithms fill whatever buffer sits at the bottleneck before they see a drop, so oversized router buffers produce hundreds of milliseconds of queueing latency (bufferbloat); the fixes are active queue management such as CoDel or FQ-CoDel at the router, ECN marking instead of dropping, or a delay-aware sender like BBR. Separately, once `cwnd` is large, dumping a full window into the network as a burst causes drops that the window size alone does not justify, so Linux paces transmissions with the `fq` qdisc and BBR depends on pacing outright.
