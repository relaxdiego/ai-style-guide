TCP congestion control is a feedback loop that estimates how much data a path can hold and adjusts the estimate based on what happens to the packets. The sender maintains a congestion window (cwnd) and keeps at most `min(cwnd, receiver's advertised window)` bytes unacknowledged in flight. Nothing in the network tells the sender the right value, so the sender probes upward until it sees a loss signal, backs off, and probes again.

The classic loop, from RFC 5681, has four states:

- **Slow start.** cwnd begins at roughly 10 MSS (Linux) and grows by 1 MSS per ACK, so it doubles every round trip. Exponential growth, despite the name, which describes the starting point rather than the rate. Runs until cwnd reaches ssthresh or a loss occurs.
- **Congestion avoidance.** cwnd grows by about 1 MSS per round trip, implemented as `cwnd += MSS*MSS/cwnd` per ACK. This is the additive increase half of AIMD.
- **Fast retransmit.** Three duplicate ACKs mean a segment is missing but later ones arrived, so the sender resends immediately instead of waiting for a timer.
- **Fast recovery.** After fast retransmit, halve ssthresh and cwnd and continue in congestion avoidance rather than restarting from scratch.

Which loss signal you get determines how hard you fall:

| Signal | ssthresh | new cwnd | next state |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3 duplicate ACKs | cwnd/2 | cwnd/2 | fast recovery, then congestion avoidance |
| Retransmission timeout | cwnd/2 | 1 MSS | slow start |

The reason multiplicative decrease works is Chiu and Jain's result: additive increase with multiplicative decrease converges to a fair, efficient allocation among senders sharing a bottleneck, while additive decrease does not. The cost is a sawtooth throughput pattern and a throughput that scales as roughly `MSS/(RTT * sqrt(p))` for loss rate p, which falls apart on long fat paths where you need a huge window and a single loss cuts it in half.

That limitation drove the algorithms in use now:

- **Reno / NewReno.** The baseline above. NewReno fixes Reno's handling of multiple losses in one window by staying in fast recovery until all outstanding data is acknowledged.
- **CUBIC.** Linux default. cwnd follows a cubic function of the time since the last loss, so it climbs fast, flattens near the previous loss point (the plateau), then probes aggressively past it. Growth depends on elapsed time rather than ACK arrivals, which makes it fairer across different RTTs and scalable on high bandwidth-delay paths.
- **BBR.** Google's model-based approach. It measures the bottleneck bandwidth and minimum RTT, then paces sending at that estimate instead of reacting to loss at all. It keeps queues short and handles lossy links well, but it competes badly with loss-based senders in some configurations.
- **Vegas** and other delay-based schemes read rising RTT as an early congestion signal. They get crushed when sharing a queue with loss-based senders, which keep pushing until the buffer overflows.
- **DCTCP.** Datacenter-only, uses ECN marks to scale the decrease proportionally to the fraction of marked packets rather than always halving.

Two things sit alongside the sender algorithm. ECN lets a router mark a packet instead of dropping it, so the sender learns about congestion without losing data, and it needs support in both endpoints and the router. Bufferbloat is the failure mode of loss-based control against oversized router buffers: the sender fills a multi-second queue before it ever sees a drop, so latency collapses while throughput looks fine. Active queue management (CoDel, FQ_CoDel, PIE) fixes this in the router by dropping or marking based on how long packets sit in the queue rather than on the queue's length in bytes.
