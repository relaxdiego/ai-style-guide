TCP congestion control is a sender-side feedback loop. The sender keeps a congestion window (cwnd) counting how many bytes it may have in flight unacknowledged, grows cwnd while acknowledgements keep arriving, and cuts it when it sees a signal that the network is overloaded. There is no signal from the routers in the classic design, so the sender infers congestion from packet loss, which it detects from duplicate acknowledgements or a retransmission timeout. Flow control is a separate limit: the receiver advertises a window (rwnd) saying how much buffer it has, and the sender is bound by min(cwnd, rwnd).

The classic algorithm has four phases, all operating on cwnd measured in multiples of the maximum segment size (MSS):

- **Slow start.** cwnd begins at the initial window, 10 MSS on current Linux, and increases by 1 MSS per acknowledged segment. That doubles cwnd every round trip, so growth is exponential despite the name. It runs until cwnd reaches the slow start threshold (ssthresh) or a loss occurs.
- **Congestion avoidance.** Above ssthresh, cwnd grows by roughly 1 MSS per round trip, implemented as `cwnd += MSS*MSS/cwnd` per acknowledgement. This is the additive increase, and it probes for extra capacity slowly enough to avoid overshooting badly.
- **Fast retransmit and fast recovery.** Three duplicate acknowledgements mean a segment was lost but later ones arrived, so the path is still delivering. The sender retransmits the missing segment immediately without waiting for a timer, sets ssthresh to cwnd/2, sets cwnd to the new ssthresh, and stays in congestion avoidance. This is the multiplicative decrease.
- **Timeout.** If the retransmission timeout fires, the sender assumes the path is severely congested or broken. ssthresh drops to cwnd/2, cwnd drops to 1 MSS, and slow start begins again. The timeout also doubles on repeated failure.

Additive increase with multiplicative decrease is what makes competing flows converge on a fair share of a bottleneck: every flow adds the same absolute amount per round trip and cuts by the same proportion, so a flow with a larger share loses more in absolute terms at each cut. The resulting steady state is the sawtooth pattern you see in throughput graphs. It also gives the well-known throughput approximation of about `MSS / (RTT * sqrt(p))` for loss probability `p`, which says that a long-RTT flow loses badly to a short-RTT flow sharing the same link, and that at high bandwidth-delay products the tolerable loss rate becomes absurdly small.

That last problem is why nobody ships plain Reno any more. The variants differ mainly in what they treat as a congestion signal and how they grow the window:

| Variant | Congestion signal | Growth in congestion avoidance | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reno | 3 dup ACKs, timeout | Linear, +1 MSS/RTT | Recovers one loss per window well, stalls on multiple |
| NewReno | Same | Same | Stays in fast recovery across multiple losses in one window |
| SACK | Same, plus explicit holes | Same | Receiver reports exactly which blocks arrived, so the sender retransmits only what is missing |
| CUBIC | Loss | Cubic function of time since the last cut | Linux default, RTT-independent, fills large pipes fast |
| Vegas | Rising RTT | Linear | Backs off before queues fill, loses to loss-based flows sharing a link |
| BBR | Measured bottleneck bandwidth and minimum RTT | Paces at the estimated bandwidth | Ignores loss as a primary signal, targets low queueing delay |

Two mechanisms sit alongside the window algorithm and matter in practice. Explicit Congestion Notification lets a router mark a packet instead of dropping it, and the receiver echoes the mark back so the sender can cut its window without anyone losing data; it needs support at the sender, the receiver, and the bottleneck queue. Pacing spreads a window's worth of packets evenly across the round trip rather than releasing them as a burst, which is mandatory for BBR and helpful everywhere else. Both exist largely because deep router buffers break the loss signal: a loss-based algorithm grows cwnd until the buffer is full, so a fat buffer means the algorithm's steady state is a permanently full queue and hundreds of milliseconds of added latency for everything sharing the link.
