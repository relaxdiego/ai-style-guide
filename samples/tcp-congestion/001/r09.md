TCP congestion control is a feedback loop that guesses how much data the network path can hold and adjusts a sender-side window until packets start getting lost or delayed. The network gives no explicit capacity signal, so the sender probes upward until something breaks, backs off, and repeats.

The sender keeps a congestion window (cwnd) in bytes. Data in flight is capped by `min(cwnd, receiver's advertised window)`, so cwnd is the sender's model of the path and rwnd is the receiver's buffer limit. Since throughput is roughly cwnd divided by round-trip time, controlling cwnd controls rate.

The classic Reno control loop has four phases:

- **Slow start.** cwnd begins at ~10 MSS and doubles every RTT (one MSS added per ACK). Exponential, despite the name; it is slow only relative to blasting at line rate. Runs until cwnd hits `ssthresh` or a loss occurs.
- **Congestion avoidance.** cwnd grows by about one MSS per RTT — additive increase. This is the cautious probing phase where the connection spends most of its life.
- **Fast retransmit.** Three duplicate ACKs mean a packet is missing but later packets arrived, so the path still works. Retransmit immediately instead of waiting for the retransmission timeout.
- **Fast recovery.** Halve ssthresh and cwnd, then continue in congestion avoidance rather than dropping to 1 MSS. A timeout, by contrast, means total silence, so cwnd collapses to 1 MSS and slow start restarts.

Additive increase, multiplicative decrease (AIMD) is what makes this stable and roughly fair. Chiu and Jain showed that increasing by a constant and decreasing by a proportion drives competing flows toward an equal share; increasing multiplicatively does not converge. The cost is the sawtooth: throughput oscillates between half and full path capacity, and average utilization sits near 75%.

Reno's weakness is that halving on every loss scales badly. Throughput is bounded by roughly `MSS / (RTT · √p)` for loss rate p, so a 10 Gbps transcontinental link needs absurdly low loss to stay full, and long-RTT flows lose to short-RTT flows sharing the same bottleneck.

| Algorithm | Signal | Behavior | Where used |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reno / NewReno | Loss | AIMD, halve on loss | Baseline, still the reference |
| CUBIC | Loss | Cubic growth from last loss point; RTT-independent | Linux and Windows default |
| BBR | Bandwidth + RTT estimate | Models bottleneck rate and min RTT, paces to it; ignores loss | Google, YouTube, QUIC deployments |
| Vegas | Delay | Backs off on rising RTT before loss | Rare; loses to loss-based flows |
| DCTCP | ECN marks | Scales backoff to fraction of marked packets | Datacenters with ECN-capable switches |

Two modern wrinkles matter in practice. ECN lets a router mark a packet instead of dropping it, giving the sender the congestion signal without the retransmission; DCTCP and L4S build on this. And bufferbloat is the reason loss-based control degrades badly on paths with oversized buffers: a loss-driven sender fills the buffer before it sees a signal, adding hundreds of milliseconds of queuing delay to everything sharing the link. BBR's premise is exactly this — it targets the point where bandwidth is maximized and queuing has not yet started, rather than the point where the queue overflows.
