TCP congestion control is a feedback loop: the sender guesses how much unacknowledged data the network can hold, sends that much, and adjusts the guess based on whether packets get through. The network never tells the sender its capacity, so the sender probes upward until something breaks, then backs off.

The sender's send rate is bounded by `min(cwnd, rwnd)`, where `rwnd` is the receiver's advertised buffer and `cwnd` is the congestion window the sender maintains itself. Everything below is about how `cwnd` moves.

**The classic loop (Reno / NewReno)**

| Phase | Trigger | cwnd behavior |
|---|---|---|
| Slow start | Connection open, or RTO | Doubles every RTT (`+1 MSS` per ACK) until `cwnd >= ssthresh` |
| Congestion avoidance | `cwnd >= ssthresh` | `+1 MSS` per RTT — additive increase |
| Fast retransmit | 3 duplicate ACKs | Resend the missing segment without waiting for a timeout |
| Fast recovery | After fast retransmit | `ssthresh = cwnd/2`, `cwnd = ssthresh`, resume congestion avoidance |
| Timeout (RTO) | No ACK at all | `ssthresh = cwnd/2`, `cwnd = 1 MSS`, back to slow start |

This is AIMD — additive increase, multiplicative decrease. It converges to fairness between competing flows and, in aggregate, keeps the network stable. The sawtooth you see in a throughput graph is the increase probing for more bandwidth and the decrease reacting to loss.

**What counts as a congestion signal**

- **Packet loss.** Duplicate ACKs mean one segment vanished but later ones arrived. An RTO means the path may be gone entirely, hence the drastic reset.
- **Delay.** Rising RTT means queues are filling. Vegas and BBR act on this; Reno and CUBIC ignore it.
- **ECN marks.** Routers set a bit in the IP header instead of dropping. DCTCP uses the *fraction* of marked packets to scale its cut, rather than always halving.

**Modern algorithms**

| Algorithm | Signal | Cut on loss | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reno / NewReno | Loss | 50% | The reference. Poor on high bandwidth-delay paths |
| CUBIC | Loss | ~30% | Linux default. `cwnd` follows a cubic curve in time since last loss, so it plateaus near the old max then probes aggressively past it. Independent of RTT, so it's fairer across mixed-latency flows |
| BBR | Bandwidth + RTT estimates | Not loss-driven | Models the path's delivery rate and min RTT, paces at that rate. Avoids filling queues, so it dodges bufferbloat, but can be unfair to CUBIC flows |
| Vegas | Delay | — | Backs off before loss. Loses badly when competing with loss-based flows |
| DCTCP | ECN fraction | Proportional | Datacenter only; requires switch support and shallow-threshold marking |

**Things that bite in practice**

- **Bufferbloat.** Oversized router buffers mean loss-based algorithms fill hundreds of milliseconds of queue before they get their signal. This is why latency spikes during a large upload. AQM (fq_codel, CAKE) at the bottleneck is the fix, not a sender-side change.
- **Wireless loss.** Reno and CUBIC read radio corruption as congestion and cut the window for no reason. BBR handles this better.
- **Initial window.** RFC 6928 raised it to 10 segments, which is why short HTTP responses often complete inside slow start and never reach congestion avoidance.
- **Congestion control is separate from flow control.** `rwnd` protects the receiver's memory; `cwnd` protects the network. Both are enforced, and the smaller one wins.
