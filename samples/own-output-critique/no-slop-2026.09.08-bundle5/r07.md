The difference is that I optimize for the code being correct when you read it, and you need it to be correct when you change it. Those produce different code, and the gap doesn't show up on any line.

Concretely, here is what I do that passes review and hurts later:

- **I write to the spec you gave, not to the one you have.** You asked for three retry cases, so I wrote a function that handles exactly three. A person who knew the domain would have written the version that handles the fourth without being touched, because they know the fourth is coming. I don't know it's coming, so my version is a correct implementation of a requirement that expires.
- **I put things where they are locally reasonable rather than where they belong.** A helper goes next to its only caller. It reads fine. Six months later there are four callers in three modules and the helper still lives in the first one, because nothing ever forced the question. I never establish the boundary that would have made the right home obvious, since I'm not the one paying to look for it.
- **I make every abstraction the same size.** Real codebases have a few deep, load-carrying pieces and a lot of thin ones. I tend to produce uniform middle-weight functions, so nothing tells you what matters. You lose the ability to skim, and every part of the file demands equal attention.
- **My names describe the implementation, not the intent.** `processUserData` is accurate and tells you nothing about why it exists or what would make it wrong. When you change the behaviour, the name still fits, so it stops being a check on anything.
- **I don't leave the reasoning that would let you undo the decision.** I choose a data structure, it works, and I write no comment because the code is clear. But the comment you need is not what it does, it's why not the other thing, and what would have to change for the other thing to win. Without that, every one of my choices is an unopened box, and you have to re-derive the tradeoff before you can touch it.

What you can do about it: tell me the change you expect next, not just the behaviour you want now, and tell me which parts of the system are allowed to know about each other. Those two constraints are what a maintainer carries in their head and what I have no way to infer from the request.
