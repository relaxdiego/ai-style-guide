Nothing is wrong at the line level, which is why you can't point at a line. What's missing is the information a maintainer normally reads between the lines, and review can't catch its absence because review compares the diff to the request.

- Every line arrives with the same weight. In code that grew, the ugly conditional has an outage behind it and the tidy part was never touched, so you can tell what's fragile by what looks scarred. I write it all in one pass. Nothing is marked as hard-won, nothing is marked as a guess, and when you go to change it you don't know which branch will page someone.
- I check for errors evenly rather than where they occur. Someone who has run the system is paranoid at the trust boundary and trusting inside it, so a nil check there is a claim that the value can really be nil. I check everywhere, which makes every check noise and buries the one that was telling you something.
- I complete patterns that were never real. Three handlers, two of which need retry logic, and I give retry logic to all three or extract a base class. Those three rhymed on the day I wrote them. When one diverges, the abstraction fights whoever has to change it.
- My names summarise the body. `validateInput` and `processItems` are accurate and tell you nothing you wouldn't get from reading two lines down. A maintainer writes `rejectStaleQuote`, which asserts what must not happen.
- I leave no gaps. A hole in human code that means "we decided not to support this" is information. I will half-support the case quietly, so the limit shows up in production rather than in the source.
- My comments state intent I inferred, not intent I know. They read as authoritative and they go stale as lies rather than as omissions.
- Each function is locally good and the set has no single theory. Where state lives, who validates, who owns the lifetime: I decide per function from whatever was on screen. Changing one behaviour later touches six files that are each fine.
- My tests come from the implementation, written minutes after it, so they encode its structure. They pass forever and they break during refactors for reasons unrelated to behaviour.
- I write a new helper rather than find yours. Each duplicate is defensible alone.

Three things change this, and only you can supply them: the invariant and the incident behind the code before I write it, permission to mark what I guessed instead of writing every line at full confidence, and a question at the end about what I chose not to handle. The last one is the cheapest. I always made those choices and I rarely mention them.
