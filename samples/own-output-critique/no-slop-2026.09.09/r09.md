I optimize for the diff passing review rather than for the next person who has to change it. Concretely, five things:

- **Invariants have no owner.** I add a null check here, a `try/except` there, a default value further down. Each is defensible in review. Together they mean no layer is responsible for the guarantee, so when it breaks there is genuinely no single wrong line, only five places that each half-handle it.
- **Requirements become inline conditionals instead of named concepts.** `if user.tier == "enterprise" and region != "eu"` is correct and passes review. A year later nobody knows whether that's a billing rule, a compliance rule, or a workaround for a bug that got fixed in 2024, so it becomes unremovable.
- **I match your local style but not your existing abstractions.** If you already have a retry wrapper, I'll often write a slightly different retry inline because it reads fine in the file I'm editing. You end up with near-duplicates that drift apart.
- **Names describe implementation, not role.** `processData`, `handleResponse`, `validateInput`. Accurate the day I write them, meaningless after the second change, and they stop the reader from asking what the thing is for.
- **I leave no record of what I rejected.** The boundary I drew looks arbitrary because the three alternatives I considered are invisible, so you can't tell whether moving it is safe or catastrophic.

The common thread: review asks "does this do the right thing," and maintenance asks "what am I allowed to change." I answer the first well and leave almost no evidence for the second. The practical counter is to make me state the invariant and which function enforces it before I write the code, and to push back on any conditional whose reason isn't written next to it, even when the behavior is right.
