Most of what makes it painful later is a decision I made silently and never marked as a decision. Review reads the lines that exist; the damage is in what those lines don't tell you, so there's nothing to point at.

The specific mechanisms:

- **I resolve ambiguity without saying I did.** Your spec was underdetermined in six places. I picked something plausible in each and wrote it in the same confident register as the parts you actually specified. Six months on, nobody can tell which behavior was required and which I invented, so nobody will change any of it.
- **Constants have no provenance.** My `timeout=30` and `max_retries=3` are plausible numbers with no incident behind them. A human's 30 came from an outage. Mine looks identical on the page and is worth nothing, but you'll treat both as tuned.
- **Defensive checks everywhere instead of one invariant.** I re-validate and re-normalize at every level because each function should be correct alone. That reads as careful. The cost: there's no single place where the invariant gets established, so no check can ever be safely deleted, and the count only goes up.
- **Parallel structure where the behavior isn't parallel.** Three handlers with matching signatures because matching looks clean. When one of them genuinely needs to diverge, the person doing it has to either break the pattern or contort the code into it, and they'll pick the contortion.
- **I re-derive instead of reusing.** A second date parser, a slightly different retry helper, a near-duplicate error type. Each passes review. Now there are three ways to do one thing and no stated rule about which is canonical.
- **Names taken from the local line, not your domain.** `result`, `processed_items`, `handle_data`. Nothing is wrong at the line. But the domain word never enters the codebase, so grep stops finding things and the concept has no name anyone can say out loud.
- **Uniform error handling.** Everything caught, logged, re-raised the same way, which erases the ranking of which failures must wake someone and which must stay quiet. Real systems need that ranking, and flattening it is invisible in review.
- **Tests written against my implementation.** I test what I just wrote, so coverage is green and the tests pin current behavior rather than intended behavior. Every refactor becomes a test rewrite, which teaches people not to refactor.
- **I add rather than restructure.** A small diff reviews well. Over many sessions the file accretes layers that each made sense against the file as it was that day, and no one ever paid the restructuring cost.

What actually fixes it: make me write down the choices, not just the code. Ask for a short list of every ambiguity I resolved and what I assumed, require a comment giving the source of any magic number (or `# arbitrary, tune under load` when it is arbitrary), tell me to name the invariant and enforce it at one boundary instead of checking everywhere, and have me write the test list from your description before I write the implementation. The last one is the highest yield, since tests derived from the spec catch the places where my guess and your intent differ.
