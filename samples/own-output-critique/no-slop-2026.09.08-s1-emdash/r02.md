Every line looks equally deliberate, so you can't tell which ones are decisions and which are defaults.

Concretely, the things that pass review and hurt later:

- **Ambiguity resolved silently.** Where the requirement was underspecified, you'd ask or leave a TODO. I pick a reading and implement it cleanly. The open question becomes an invisible commitment that reads, six months on, like a deliberate rule someone chose.
- **Constants that look measured.** `maxRetries = 3`, 30s timeout, batch size 500, 4096-byte buffer. Nothing was benchmarked; those are just the common values. You can't tune them later because you can't tell whether 3 was load-tested or typed.
- **Uniform care.** I handle the impossible branch as thoroughly as the hot path. Your own code is terse where it's safe and paranoid where it once burned someone, and that unevenness is how a maintainer finds the dangerous parts by skimming. Mine is flat, so skimming tells you nothing.
- **Abstraction at one caller.** A helper, an interface, or a config parameter introduced because it looks like good structure, not because a second caller forced it. The generality has no user, and you can't delete it without proving a negative.
- **Names accurate about the code, silent about the domain.** `validateInput`, `processRecord`, `ResultHandler`. Nothing is wrong, so review passes. Compare `rejectPostCutoffOrders`, which leaks a business rule you'd otherwise have to reconstruct.
- **Tests derived from the implementation.** I wrote the code, then wrote assertions describing what it does. They pass forever, fail on every refactor, and catch no bugs.
- **Local consistency, global divergence.** I match whatever file is in front of me. The same concept ends up with three idioms across three files, and each diff was defensible on its own.

The fix is to make me expose the parts that were guesses rather than reasoned. Ask for an assumptions list alongside the diff, naming every place the spec was ambiguous and what I picked; require that any constant either cite a measurement or be marked as arbitrary; ban new abstractions until a second caller exists; and have tests written against the requirement before the implementation, or by a separate pass that never sees the code.
