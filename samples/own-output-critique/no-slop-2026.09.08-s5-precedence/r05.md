The missing thing is not in any line, which is why you can't point at one. Each line is defensible on its own. What's absent is the record of what was decided: which states are impossible, which alternative was rejected and why, which parts are deliberate and which are arbitrary. Maintenance is mostly editing decisions, and you can't edit a decision that was never written down.

Concrete mechanisms, roughly in order of how much they cost you later:

- **Defensive checks against states that can't happen.** I add `if not items: return []` and a null guard on a field the constructor guarantees. Each check passes review. Together they destroy your ability to read what the function actually assumes, so every future change starts with re-deriving the invariant from scratch.
- **No stance on a tradeoff.** I pick the unbounded cache, the O(n²) scan, the synchronous call, and say nothing. A human would have written "N is under 100 here" or "batched because the API rate-limits at 10/s". Without that, you can't tell whether changing it is safe or whether you're about to reintroduce a bug I fixed.
- **Written against the prompt, not the codebase.** I produce a fourth retry helper with slightly different backoff, or a date parser next to your existing one. Review checks whether the code is correct, not whether it is the third copy.
- **Comments that restate the code.** The loop gets a comment saying it iterates over users. The reason the sort has to be stable, which is the only thing you can't recover by reading, is not there.
- **Names for the local step instead of the domain.** `process_data`, `handle_result`, `enrich`. You can't grep for the concept and you can't distinguish two of them six months later.
- **Errors caught and flattened.** try/except, log, return None. Every caller then has to guess whether None means empty, missing, or broken, and the original failure context is gone.
- **Tests pinned to the implementation.** Asserts on mock call order and internal methods. They pass, they break on every refactor, and they catch nothing. This one is expensive and review almost never flags it.
- **Structure sized to the request.** A generic parameterized wrapper with one caller, or a 200-line function left inline, depending on how you phrased the ask. The module boundaries end up nowhere near where your changes actually land, because I have no evidence about where they land and don't ask.
- **Consistency at the wrong scale.** Each chunk I write is internally consistent and reads like a different author from the file two directories over.

What changes the output: tell me to state invariants as asserts or types instead of guarding them, to leave a comment only where a real choice was made, to find and reuse the existing helper before writing one (name the module if you know it), and to test observable behavior with real objects rather than mocks. Also tell me when a function is allowed to assume its inputs are valid. Absent that instruction I default to defending against everything, and that default is the largest single source of the feeling you're describing.
