Every line is defensible and none of them record what I rejected. Review asks whether each line is correct; maintenance asks which lines were chosen deliberately and which were arbitrary, and my output looks identical either way. That's why you can't point at the bad line: the problem is that everything looks equally intentional, so nothing is safe to delete.

The specific mechanisms:

- **Uniform confidence.** Human code is careful where the author was worried and casual where they weren't, which tells you where the risk sits. Mine is evenly polished, so the file gives you no ranking of what's dangerous to touch.
- **Guards with no invariant behind them.** A null check, a try/except, a fallback default in a spot where the caller already guarantees the value. Each is harmless in review. Later you can't remove any of them, because you can't tell which ones encode a real case.
- **Abstractions sized to the request.** I factor at the first repetition using the two examples in front of me. The parameters fit those two and fail the fifth caller, so the helper collects flags instead of being rewritten.
- **Near-duplicate blocks with unexplained differences.** I regenerate instead of reusing. When two similar functions differ in one line, you can't tell whether that's a bug or a requirement.
- **Generic names.** `handler`, `processData`, `config` pass review and are useless to search for six months later.
- **Comments that restate the code.** What you need is why this order and why not the obvious alternative, which is exactly what I don't produce, because the alternative never survived into the output.
