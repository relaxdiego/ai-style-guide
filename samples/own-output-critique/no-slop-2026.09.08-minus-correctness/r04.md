The wrongness is usually in the decomposition and in the missing reasons, not in any statement. Review checks statements.

Concrete mechanisms, in rough order of how often they bite:

- **Arbitrary boundaries.** I split functions and modules to satisfy the request in front of me, not around a model of what the system is for. Each piece is defensible alone; the set of pieces matches no decomposition you'd have chosen. When you go to change behavior, the change lands across four files that had no reason to be four files.
- **No record of what was decided.** I pick a timeout, a retry count, an ordering, a lock scope, and the alternatives I rejected vanish. Six months later every constant looks equally deliberate, so you can't tell which three values are constrained by something real and which twenty are the first thing that worked. That's the feeling of not being able to point at the line: uniform confidence, zero signal.
- **Local style matching, global drift.** I imitate whichever file I read most recently. Two modules end up with different idioms for the same job, both fine in isolation, and now there's no single answer to "how do we do X here."
- **Symmetry and defensive completeness.** I write the setter because I wrote the getter, handle all six enum cases because I handled one, and guard against errors that cannot occur on any path. Review reads that as thorough. Maintenance reads it as untested branches you must keep reasoning about.
- **Tests that mirror the implementation.** They assert the steps I wrote rather than the behavior you want, so they survive real bugs and break on every honest refactor. This passes review reliably because the test file looks dense and green.
- **No continuity.** A person who wrote your code carries the intent into the next change and consolidates. I re-derive a fresh model each session, so successive edits accrete. No single change is big enough to justify a cleanup, and the cleanup never happens.

What actually helps: tell me the invariants and the expected direction of change before I write, make me state assumptions and rejected alternatives in comments at the decision points, and reject any test that names an internal function. Then ask me to delete: after a feature works, a pass whose only goal is removing parameters, branches, and layers nothing calls will usually cut a third of what I produced.
