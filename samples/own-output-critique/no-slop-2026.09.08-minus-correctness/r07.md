The code carries no record of which parts were decisions. Everything I write comes out at the same confidence, so when you return to it you can't distinguish the line that had to be that way from the line where I picked one of three plausible options and moved on. A human author leaves those marks without trying: a hedging comment, an oddly placed guard, a helper extracted at exactly the point they got confused. Review checks correctness, correctness is there, nothing gets flagged.

The specific forms it takes:

- **Duplicated policy.** I satisfy the local spec and re-implement a rule that already lives elsewhere in your codebase. Both copies are correct on review day. Now there are two places to change and no link between them.
- **Guards for states that can't happen.** Each null check and fallback is individually defensible. Collectively they erase your ability to see what the real invariants are, and none of them can be deleted because nothing says why it's there.
- **Flat emphasis.** Every function named with equal care, every branch weighted the same. Your code has two or three ideas that matter and a lot of plumbing; mine doesn't tell you which is which, so every reading starts from zero.
- **Mechanism names instead of your domain's names.** `processResult` is accurate and impossible to grep by concept. You say "settlement," I say "handler."
- **Tests that mirror my implementation** instead of your requirement. They pass, and they protect nothing the moment you restructure.

The fix that actually works: give me the invariants and the domain vocabulary before I write, and tell me to flag guesses inline. I'll mark them if asked; I won't volunteer them.
