Nothing in the working directory to look at, so this is from the general pattern rather than your code in particular.

The difference is that the code carries no record of the decisions that produced it. You can't point at the line because what's missing was never in a line: it's the trace of what was considered and rejected, what's load-bearing, and what was arbitrary.

Human code has a gradient of care. The part someone struggled with reads differently from the part they banged out on a Friday, and that unevenness is information. It tells a maintainer where the danger was, which weird conditional is a scar from a real incident, and which block is safe to delete. I write at one level of polish throughout. A null check guarding an actual production failure looks exactly like a null check I added because careful code has null checks. When you go to change something, every line has an equal claim on being important, so you either preserve all of it or you gamble, and both of those are expensive in a way that never shows up in review.

Unearned defensiveness makes this worse, because every `try`/`except` and `or {}` is a claim about what can happen. When I add them for texture rather than because the value can really be absent, the code stops being readable as a statement of its own invariants. You can no longer tell from the call site whether that argument is ever `None`. The fallback also converts a real bug into a plausible wrong answer, which you find three weeks later in data rather than in a stack trace.

The second thing is that I optimise locally and commit globally to nothing. Each decision gets made fresh at the point of use and is consistent with the ten lines around it, which is exactly the window review looks at. Across a system this accumulates several near-synonymous ways to do one thing: two error conventions, three slightly different notions of what a user is, retry logic at two layers that mostly agree. No individual diff is wrong. The defect is that there's no longer a single answer to "where does this belong", and that question is most of what maintenance actually is.

Related, and probably the one you feel most: my abstractions have no pressure behind them. A person extracts a helper because two callers already diverged and the divergence hurt. I introduce one because the situation resembles situations that usually have one. It wraps a single caller, it's clean, it's tested, it reviews well, and its seam doesn't lie along any axis the system actually varies on. Then the first real requirement arrives cutting across the seam, and you have to take the abstraction apart before you can do the work. Reading it, nothing is wrong. Changing it, everything is in the way.

Finally, the "why" is systematically absent. My comments tend to restate the mechanism, which you could have read off the code, rather than record the reason or the alternative I discarded. My names are descriptive but drawn from generic engineering vocabulary rather than your domain's, so they don't connect to how you and your colleagues talk about the system, and the mapping has to be rebuilt in the reader's head every time. And the tests get written from the implementation rather than from what has to be true, so they cover the lines and re-encode the same misunderstanding, and the safety net fails to hold weight at precisely the moment you lean on it, which is a refactor.

What changes this in practice is reviewing my code for "how would I change this" rather than "is this correct", deleting speculative abstractions on sight rather than letting them earn tenure, and making me write down what I rejected and why when a decision was close. Telling me your conventions explicitly also helps more than it should, because otherwise I infer them from the nearest file and the inference is only ever local.
