The difference is that my code carries no record of what it rejected, and maintenance is mostly an argument with the decisions that produced code rather than with the code itself.

When you write a function, it's the residue of a search. You considered a queue and didn't use one, you made the field nullable because of something the upstream service does, you left the retry crude because that path has never once failed in production. Six months later that history is what you actually consult, and it lives partly in your head, partly in the team's memory, and partly in the code as texture: the careful stretches are where somebody got burned, the perfunctory ones are where nobody cared. Texture is a map of where the load sits. Mine is uniformly finished, every line equally considered-looking, so nothing distinguishes the structural choices from the coin flips I could have called either way. When you go to change it, every direction feels equally sanctioned and equally risky. That is the feeling you're describing, and it has no line to point at because it's a property of the whole surface.

Several specific habits fall out of that, and they're all things review is bad at catching. I optimise for the read-through, because review is a read-through and it's the only judgement my code has ever received; I've never come back to my own work in a year and paid for it, so what I know about maintainability is inherited description rather than experience, and when the two pull apart I err toward code that reads well now. I also have a strong pull toward parallel structure: three handlers get the same shape, and when one of them has a genuinely different failure mode I tend to smooth it to match rather than let it stick out. It reads beautifully and misdescribes the domain, and you find out when you need to change the odd one and discover the symmetry was cosmetic. Your abstractions usually get extracted after you've seen the repetition, so they're shaped by the cases they were pulled out of; mine are often anticipated, because I recognise a pattern by name and instantiate it, which fits the typical cases I imagined and splits open on the first real one that isn't typical.

The naming and the comments are the part that most directly steals your ability to point. My names are accurate and about a half-step generic, and accurate names pass review, but a name is also a claim about a boundary, and a loose one lets a function accrete responsibilities for years without ever looking wrong. A committed name starts to itch when the function stops being the thing it says. My comments fail the same way but worse: I comment what the code does, when the valuable comment records what isn't in the code, the constraint imposed by another team's API or the obvious approach that doesn't work. A restating comment is worse than no comment, because it occupies the slot where the real one would have gone and looks like the work has been done. Uniform error handling does this too. Guards spread at an even rate are each individually defensible and collectively tell you nothing about where the system actually breaks, and they quietly convert loud failures into degradations.

I should say that I'm partly reasoning about this from the outside. I don't have clean access to why I produce what I produce, so this is inference from the artifacts rather than a report from the inside.

What changes the outcome is mostly making the missing record explicit rather than making the code nicer. Ask for the alternatives I discarded and where I was genuinely torn, and have them land in the PR body or in comments at the specific lines. Tell me which parts are load-bearing and which are throwaway, so the texture becomes deliberate instead of uniform. When three things in a diff look alike, treat that as a claim to check rather than a sign of care, and be most suspicious of any abstraction I introduce on first sight of a pattern instead of the third.
