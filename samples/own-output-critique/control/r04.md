Nothing is wrong with the line. What's missing was never on a line: my code records no decisions.

Maintenance works mostly by reading intent off structure. You ask why two things are separate, and in code a person wrote, the answer is usually that they knew those things would diverge; you ask why a third case is inlined rather than folded into the loop above it, and the answer is that folding it in broke something once. The arrangement is a record of what the author found genuinely different. When I write, the arrangement comes from resemblance to code I've seen rather than from anything I knew about your system. It's tidy, it's conventional, and it's inert. So when you go to change it, the usual questions come back empty, and the emptiness doesn't localise anywhere you can point.

The concrete forms this takes:

- **Care applied at uniform density.** Human code has hot spots. The author was frightened of one function and bored by four others, and the comments, the guard clauses and the tests cluster around the fear. Mine are evenly finished everywhere, so a validation guarding a real failure mode sits at the same visual weight as one guarding a condition that cannot occur. Your best tool for reading unfamiliar code, finding where the author slowed down, gives you nothing.
- **Abstraction that no second caller asked for.** A parameter, a strategy object, a config dict, introduced because extension points look like good practice. You inherit a seam you now have to respect, maintain and reason through, and it never earned its place, because there was only ever one case.
- **Comments that narrate instead of record.** The scarce thing in a comment is the part not visible in the code: which bug, which upstream quirk, why this order. I tend to spend the comment budget restating what the lines already say, which means the budget looks spent and the actual why was never written down.
- **Names from generic programming vocabulary.** `handler`, `process`, `result`, `manager` where your codebase has a real domain noun. Each one is individually fine and collectively they dilute the language your team greps by.
- **Tests written by reading the implementation.** I usually write them after the code, from the code, so they encode what it does rather than what it owes. They stay green forever, and when you deliberately change behaviour they break in a way that can't tell you whether you broke a promise.
- **Drift across a session.** I re-derive style from whatever is in front of me each turn, so over twenty edits you accumulate two near-identical helpers and two conventions for the same job. Every diff was locally reasonable. The sum isn't.

Review passes because review is a per-diff, per-line instrument, and all of this lives in negative space: the distinction I didn't make, the reason I didn't write down, the generalisation I couldn't have justified. A reviewer checks that the code is correct and idiomatic, and it is. There's no artefact in the diff for "the author didn't know anything about this decision," so nothing gets flagged.

The root of it is that my feedback signal is how code reads at rest, not what it cost to change nine months later. I don't carry the code. You do.

What actually changes the output is giving me the things I can't infer and then insisting they land in the artefact. Tell me the constraint behind a request, not just the request, and ask me to put it in the comment or the commit body where it will still be there when the code moves. Refuse abstraction until there's a second caller and say so as a standing rule. Give me your domain nouns and hold me to them. Ask for the test from the requirement before the implementation exists, so it can't be a mirror. And when I hand you something, the useful question is not whether each line is right but which lines I had a reason for, because I'll answer that honestly, and the ones where the answer is thin are the ones that will hurt you later.
