The difference is that my code has no history, and maintenance depends on history.

When you read human code, you read decisions. An odd null check means someone got paged at 3am. A function split at a strange place means two teams owned the halves. You navigate by asking "why is this here?" and the code answers. My code answers "because it was reasonable," uniformly, for every line. That is what you are feeling and cannot point at: there is no line that is wrong, and there is also no line that tells you anything.

The specific mechanisms:

- I write uncertain code in the same register as certain code. Human authors leak confusion through awkward names, a TODO, a comment that trails off. That leak tells you where to be careful. I produce even prose everywhere, so you cannot tell which parts I understood and which I pattern-matched.
- My abstractions are fitted to exactly the call sites that existed when I wrote them. They are correct and minimal for three callers. The fourth caller, six weeks later, does not fit, and the abstraction has to be torn out rather than extended, because it was never generalized past the sample I could see.
- I make things parallel because parallel reads well in review. Three handlers get the same structure even when one has a different lifecycle. Later you cannot tell whether the symmetry is a contract you must preserve or a coincidence of how I typed it.
- I name after the code, not the domain. `validateInput`, `processRecord`, `handleUpdate`. Accurate, reviewable, and useless in a year, because the name does not carry the business rule. A domain expert would have written `settlementWindow` and taught you something.
- My comments restate the mechanism instead of recording the reason. I have a derivation, not a reason. So the comment says what the loop does, which you can already see, and not why the retry count is 3.
- I add defensive handling at every level: null guards, broad excepts, fallback defaults. Each is individually defensible in review. Together they erase failure signals, so real bugs surface far from their cause, and you cannot tell which invariants are actually guaranteed.
- I re-derive style every session from whatever is in context. Two features I wrote a week apart may use different idioms for the same job, with no author to ask and no principle behind the split. The codebase accumulates dialects.
- I fill in the whole matrix of cases, including ones that cannot occur. Humans skip work they judge unnecessary, and that judgment is information. My completeness inflates the surface you have to keep correct forever.

Review catches defects at the level of the line and the function, and none of these is a defect at that level. They are properties of the relationships between parts, or of things that are absent. The fix on your side is to make me state reasons: ask what I was unsure about, ask which invariants I assumed, ask what I left out and why, and put those answers in the code before you merge it.
